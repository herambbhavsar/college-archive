# UNIT 1 — ENERGY, ENVIRONMENT AND ECOLOGY

# 1.1 Energy and the progress of human civilisation
- Every step of human progress came from a NEW way of using energy:

1. FIRE — early humans used fire for warmth, cooking and protection (striking stones).

2. ANIMAL power — farming with bullocks/oxen (ancient Egyptian ploughing).

3. WIND and WATER — windmills and water wheels for grinding and pumping.

4. STEAM / FOSSIL FUELS — the steam engine and locomotive started the Industrial Revolution (coal).

5. ELECTRICITY, oil, gas, nuclear — the modern world.
- The more advanced a civilisation, the MORE energy it uses per person — and the more it affects the environment.
- ENERGY END-USE SECTORS: where energy is finally used — households, industry, agriculture, transport, commercial buildings.

**Example:** A village family in 1900 used firewood and bullocks; a city family today uses LPG, electricity, petrol and a phone charger — all far more energy-intensive.

## Oil crisis of 1973
- In October 1973, OAPEC (Organisation of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, plus Egypt, Syria and Tunisia) declared an EMBARGO on oil production and started an oil-pricing control strategy, in response to US support for Israel in its war against Egypt.
- Result: petrol shortages in the West ("Gas shortage — sales limited to 10 gallons per customer"), price shock.
- WHY it matters for this course: the crisis made the world realise that depending on fossil fuels is risky → the search for NON-CONVENTIONAL (renewable) energy began seriously after 1973.

# 1.3 Classification of energy sources (5 ways)

| Basis | Type 1 | Type 2 |
| 1. USABILITY | PRIMARY — found in nature before any human conversion (coal, crude oil, sunlight, wind, running rivers, vegetation, radioactive material) | SECONDARY — supplied to the user after one or more conversion steps (electricity, thermal energy, hydrogen fuel) |
| 2. TRADITIONAL USE | CONVENTIONAL — used for decades, common around the 1973 oil crisis (fossil fuels, nuclear, large hydro) | NON-CONVENTIONAL — considered for large-scale use AFTER 1973 (solar, wind, biomass) |
| 3. LONG-TERM AVAILABILITY | NON-RENEWABLE — finite, not replenished after use (fossil fuels, uranium) | RENEWABLE — essentially inexhaustible (wind, solar, geothermal, tidal, hydro) |
| 4. COMMERCIAL APPLICATION | COMMERCIAL — sold in the market at a definite price (electricity, coal, refined petroleum) | NON-COMMERCIAL — not bought in a market (firewood, agro-waste in villages, solar water heating, animal power for transport, irrigation, sugarcane crushing) |
| 5. ORIGIN | Fossil fuel, nuclear, hydro, solar, wind, biomass, geothermal, tidal, ocean thermal, ocean wave energy |   |

**Example:** Petrol in your bike = primary source crude oil → secondary (refined), conventional, non-renewable, commercial. Cow-dung cakes in a village kitchen = primary, non-conventional (biomass), renewable, non-commercial.

# 1.4 Non-conventional energy sources — merits and demerits

| Merits | Demerits |
| Available in nature FREE of cost | Energy is DILUTE (spread thin) — need large areas/devices |
| No or very little POLLUTION | Harnessing cost is generally HIGH (even though the source is free) |

# 1.5 Energy–environment trade-off, ecology and the greenhouse effect

## Trade-off between energy and environment
- ENVIRONMENT literally means SURROUNDINGS.
- In any energy conversion, some energy is released to the surroundings as HEAT, and some POLLUTANTS are produced as by-products → environmental degradation.
- While meeting rising energy demand we must minimise this degradation. The present trend is a TRADE-OFF between the two; the future favours RENEWABLE, environment-friendly sources.
- 5 June is observed as WORLD ENVIRONMENT DAY to create public awareness.

## Ecology
- ECOLOGY deals with the relationship between LIVING ORGANISMS and their ENVIRONMENT.
- Nature has a SELF-CLEANING capacity and RECYCLES its resources, keeping a state of EQUILIBRIUM — e.g., the water cycle, nitrogen cycle, carbon cycle.
- When human interference EXCEEDS these limits, ecological balance is DISTURBED.

## Greenhouse effect
- NATURAL greenhouse effect: some of the sun's heat re-radiated by the earth is trapped by atmospheric gases — this keeps the earth warm enough for life.
- HUMAN-ENHANCED greenhouse effect: extra greenhouse gases trap MORE heat, so less escapes to space → global warming.
- Greenhouse gases (GHG): carbon dioxide (CO2), methane, nitrous oxide (N2O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs), sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), ozone.

**Example:** A car parked in the sun gets very hot inside — sunlight enters through glass but the heat cannot escape. The atmosphere with extra CO2 behaves like that glass.

# UNIT 2 — WHAT IS SUSTAINABLE DESIGN?

# 2.1 Definition and importance
- SUSTAINABLE DESIGN = the purposeful design of buildings, places (and products) that:

1. MINIMISE harmful environmental impacts,

2. DRASTICALLY REDUCE carbon emissions,

3. POSITIVELY INCREASE health, wellbeing, nature and biodiversity, and social value — throughout their LIFECYCLES.
- WHY IS IT IMPORTANT? Because buildings and products consume most energy and materials over their life; designing them well reduces emissions, waste and costs, and improves people's health.
- It is linked with the UN's 17 SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT GOALS (SDGs) — e.g., SDG 7 affordable & clean energy, SDG 11 sustainable cities, SDG 12 responsible consumption & production, SDG 13 climate action.

**Example:** A naturally lit, cross-ventilated college studio with rooftop solar lowers the electricity bill, keeps students healthier and cuts carbon — sustainable design in action.

# 2.2 Principles of sustainable design

## Six principles

| Principle | Meaning | Example |
| 1. OPTIMISE SITE POTENTIAL | Choose and use the site wisely — orientation, sun, wind, existing trees, water | Orient the long side of a building north–south to reduce harsh east–west sun |
| 2. OPTIMISE ENERGY USE | Reduce demand first, then use efficient systems and renewables | Daylight + LED + rooftop solar |
| 3. PROTECT AND CONSERVE WATER | Reduce, reuse and recharge water | Rainwater harvesting, low-flow taps, root-zone wastewater treatment |
| 4. OPTIMISE BUILDING SPACE AND MATERIAL USE | Use less, local, recycled, low-embodied-energy materials; flexible spaces | Fly-ash bricks, reused timber |
| 5. ENHANCE INDOOR ENVIRONMENTAL QUALITY | Good daylight, fresh air, thermal comfort, non-toxic materials | Low-VOC paints, operable windows |
| 6. OPTIMISE OPERATIONAL AND MAINTENANCE PRACTICES | Design so the building/product is easy and cheap to run and maintain | Accessible service ducts, monitoring systems |

## How can it be done? — three strategies

1. DESIGN FOR PERFORMANCE — measure and design for actual energy/water performance.

2. PRIORITISE RE-USE — reuse existing buildings, components and materials before making new ones.

3. DESIGN EFFICIENCY FOR REDUCED EMBODIED CARBON — less material and lower-carbon material (EMBODIED carbon = carbon emitted while making and transporting materials).

# UNIT 3 — SOLAR ENERGY

# 3.1 Light: wave–particle duality and the photoelectric effect

## What is light?
- Light behaves BOTH as a WAVE and as a stream of PARTICLES (photons) — this is WAVE–PARTICLE DUALITY.
- WAVE nature: shown by INTERFERENCE (light through two slits makes bright and dark bands).
- PARTICLE nature: energy comes in packets called PHOTONS. Photon energy E = hν = hc/λ (h = Planck's constant, ν = frequency, c = speed of light, λ = wavelength).
- Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → MORE energy per photon. Visible light is 400 nm (violet, ~3.1 eV) to 700 nm (red, ~1.77 eV). The EM spectrum runs gamma rays → X-rays → UV → visible → infrared → radio waves.

## Photoelectric effect
- When light of high enough frequency falls on a METAL surface, ELECTRONS are ejected (photoelectrons). Explained by Albert EINSTEIN (Nobel Prize in Physics, 1921).
- Below a threshold energy no electrons come out, however bright the light. Example on the slide: potassium needs 2.0 eV — red light (1.77 eV) ejects no electrons; green (2.25 eV) ejects electrons at ~2.96×10^5 m/s; violet (3.1 eV) at ~6.22×10^5 m/s.
- This conversion of LIGHT → ELECTRICITY is the basis of solar cells.

# 3.2 Semiconductors and the solar cell

## Semiconductor
- In silicon, electrons sit in the VALENCE BAND (VB). To conduct, they must jump the BAND GAP (Eg) into the CONDUCTION BAND (CB), e.g., by thermal excitation or by absorbing a photon.
- Silicon atoms share electrons in covalent bonds (crystal lattice); adding impurities (DOPING) makes N-type (extra electrons) or P-type (extra "holes").

## Solar (PV) cell — how it works

1. A PN JUNCTION is formed between N-type and P-type silicon.

2. Sunlight (photons) knocks electrons free near the junction.

3. The junction's electric field pushes electrons and holes in opposite directions → a voltage (~0.5–0.6 V DC per cell).

4. Metal FRONT CONTACTS (thin grid) and a BACK CONTACT collect the current to power a load (e.g., a bulb).

## Solar cell construction
- Front metallic GRID, ANTIREFLECTIVE coating, thin N-type diffused layer (~0.2 µm), thick P-type base (~300 µm), opaque back metal contact.
- MONOCRYSTALLINE cells: single crystal, dark/black, rounded corners, higher efficiency. POLYCRYSTALLINE cells: many crystals, blue, square, cheaper, slightly less efficient.

## Solar PV module (layers, top to bottom)

GLASS COVER → EVA (encapsulant) → SOLAR CELLS (connected by contacts) → EVA → BACK COVER (Tedlar).
- EVA seals and protects the cells; Tedlar protects the back from moisture; glass lets light in and protects from weather.

**Example:** The 0.5 V from one cell is too small, so 36 or 60 cells are connected in series inside one module — that is the rooftop "panel" you see.

# 3.3 Solar thermal systems (domestic)

Solar THERMAL systems use the sun's HEAT directly (not electricity). Domestic uses: heating water and homes, cooking, charging batteries, running fans, heating swimming pools.

| System | How it works (key parts) |
| SOLAR WATER HEATER | A tilted COLLECTOR (flat plate or evacuated tubes) heats water; hot water rises by natural circulation (thermosiphon) into an INSULATED hot-water tank; a COLD-WATER make-up tank refills; a vent releases pressure |
| SOLAR SPACE HEATING (passive) | Sun passes through DOUBLE GLAZING onto a dark THERMAL STORAGE WALL (Trombe wall); heated air rises and enters the room at the top, cool room air returns at the bottom → CONVECTION loop; DAMPERS control flow; a reflector can increase gain |
| SOLAR SPACE COOLING | A glazed south wall/solar chimney heats air which rises and is DUCTED OUTSIDE, pulling cool outside air into the living space (stack ventilation); black-painted chimney, operable louvres |
| SOLAR AIR HEATING SYSTEM (active) | Solar AIR HEATER (collector) → hot air stored in a ROCK-BED STORAGE → blower distributes; auxiliary heater, dampers, 3-way valve, heat exchanger for fresh air |
| SOLAR COOLING (absorption) | Solar heat drives the GENERATOR of an absorption chiller: generator → condenser (+ cooling tower) → expansion valve → EVAPORATOR (cools the space) → ABSORBER (strong/weak solution) → back; e.g., solar-powered fridge cart |
| SOLAR COOKERS | BOX type: insulated box, blackened aluminium tray, GLASS COVER, REFLECTOR mirror on the lid, black cooking pots. COMMUNITY type: large parabolic dish reflectors concentrating sunlight on a pot |

**Example:** Most Indian hostels and hotels use evacuated-tube solar water heaters on the roof — free hot water for bathing.

# UNIT 4 — WIND ENERGY

# 4.1 Origin and distribution of wind

## Origin of wind
- Wind is caused by UNEVEN HEATING of the earth by the sun (hot air rises, cooler air flows in) plus the earth's ROTATION.
- GLOBAL (planetary) winds: large circulation cells — HADLEY circulation near the equator, trade winds between the equator and 30°N/30°S, ROSSBY circulation in middle latitudes.
- LOCAL winds: caused by local differences — land and sea breezes, mountain and valley winds.

## Factors affecting the distribution of wind energy

1. Great MOUNTAINS influence air circulation at the planetary level.

2. SURFACE ROUGHNESS / friction — buildings, trees and land surface resist the air and change the wind.

3. CLIMATIC disturbances — thunderclouds and precipitation affect local winds.

4. CHANNELLING — wind speed INCREASES when passing through narrow mountain gaps.

## Variation of wind speed with height
- WIND SHEAR = the rate of change of wind speed with height.
- Near the ground, friction slows the wind; speed increases with height until shear forces become zero at the GRADIENT HEIGHT (typically about 2000 m).
- Above the gradient height = FREE ATMOSPHERE (wind not affected by the ground).
- Ground to gradient height = PLANETARY BOUNDARY LAYER, made of: (i) SURFACE LAYER up to about 100 m, and (ii) EKMAN LAYER from 100 m to the gradient height.

**Example:** This is why wind turbines are mounted on TALL towers (80–120 m) — the wind is faster and steadier higher up.

# 4.2 Wind turbine speed characteristics
- Every wind turbine–generator has:

1. CUT-IN speed — the speed at which it STARTS generating power (about 3–5 m/s).

2. RATED speed — the speed at which it reaches its RATED (full) power.

3. FURLING (cut-out) speed — the speed at which it STOPS generating to protect itself (about 25 m/s).

| Region | What happens |
| Low-speed region (below cut-in) | No power |
| Maximum power point region (cut-in → rated) | Power rises steeply — power is proportional to V³ (cube of wind speed) |
| Constant power region (rated → furling) | Power kept constant at the rated value |
| Furling region (above cut-out) | Turbine shut down |

## Power–duration curve (energy over a year)
- Plots power against the number of hours per year (8760 h) the wind exceeds each speed.
- A′, B′, C′ = power outputs at cut-in, rated and cut-out speeds.
- Area GAF is UNTAPPED — wind below cut-in speed.
- Area CDOE is UNTAPPED — wind above furling speed.
- Segment BC: wind above rated speed but power is held constant, so the extra energy (area BCB″) is also NOT harnessed.
- The HATCHED area under the output power-duration curve = the actual ANNUAL ENERGY OUTPUT of that machine at that site.

**Example:** Because P ∝ V³, doubling wind speed from 5 to 10 m/s gives 8 times more power — so site selection matters enormously.

# 4.3 Applications of wind power

## Mechanical power
- Sail-assisted cargo ships (rigid modern sails), traditional WATER-PUMPING windmills, wind-driven heating of homes.

## Electrical power
- GRID-connected system: wind turbine → DC → INVERTER → AC → utility meter → grid / building circuits.
- STAND-ALONE system: 3-blade rotor + tail (to face the wind) + generator on a tower → power storage (battery) → loads such as lights, radio, pump.

## Stand-alone examples

| Scale | Examples from the slides |
| Small-scale | Windmill next to a remote home; community water supply in Niama district, Oujda, NE Morocco; remote weather station for the Finnish coastguard; windmill for stock (cattle) water; windmill assembly at an Antarctic research hut; a boat powered by a wind turbine |
| Intermediate-scale | Ten wind turbines powering a remote village in Kotzebue, Alaska; wind/PV hybrid system in the Caribbean islands; Bergey Excel 10 kW turbine in Xiao Qing Dao island village (China); village electrification system on Krasnoe Island, Russia |

## Wind farms (large scale)
- Altamont Pass wind farm (California, USA); Texas Horse Hollow wind farm — 735 MW; offshore wind farm in Denmark.

## Hybrid wind + PV energy conversion system

PV array → DC/DC converter, and wind turbine → AC/DC converter → common DC bus → CHARGE CONTROLLER ↔ STORAGE BATTERY → DC loads (and a DUMP load for excess) + DC/AC inverter → AC loads.
- Why hybrid? Sun and wind often peak at different times (sunny calm days, windy nights/monsoon) → more reliable supply with a smaller battery.

**Example:** A remote hill-station homestay in Ladakh could use solar for daytime and a small wind turbine for windy nights, sharing one battery bank.

# UNIT 5 — ENERGY-EFFICIENT (CLIMATE-RESPONSIVE) BUILDING DESIGN

# 5.1 Climate zones and the design approach
- Energy-efficient design means using the CLIMATE (sun, wind, shade, earth, water) to keep buildings comfortable with minimum energy — PASSIVE measures first (form, orientation, insulation, shading, ventilation), then ACTIVE systems (solar water heating, PV, solar air heaters, efficient cooling).
- India's climatic zones used in the case studies: COLD & CLOUDY (Shimla), COMPOSITE (Delhi/Gurgaon — hot summer, cold winter, monsoon), WARM & HUMID (Kolkata). (India also has hot-dry and temperate zones.)

| Climate | Main goal | Typical strategies |
| Cold & cloudy | Gain and keep HEAT in winter | South-facing glazing/sunspaces, solar walls, solar air heaters, insulation, double glazing, air-lock lobbies, minimal north openings |
| Composite | Cut summer heat, admit winter sun | Orientation, shading that blocks high summer sun but admits low winter sun, insulation, courtyards, evaporative cooling, earth-air tunnels |
| Warm & humid | Avoid heat gain, maximise VENTILATION, control humidity | Shading, cross-ventilation, light/raised roofs, vegetation and water bodies, daylighting without glare |

# 5.2 Cold and cloudy climate — Shimla case studies

## Himurja Office Building, Shimla
- Office for the Himachal Pradesh Energy Development Agency (HIMURJA), with ACTIVE and PASSIVE solar RETROFITS. Architects: Arvind Krishan and Kunal Jain. Completed 1997. Built-up area 635 m².
- Cost: initial Rs 7 million; extra Rs 1.3 million for solar measures = 18.6% increase (high because the systems were RETROFITTED onto an existing building — designing them in from the start is cheaper).

| Design feature | Purpose |
| Air-heating panels integrated in the SOUTH wall | Heat gain; heated air distributed through a CONVECTIVE LOOP using the STAIRWELL |
| Double-glazed windows with proper sealing | Minimise infiltration (cold air leaking in) |
| Insulated RCC diaphragm walls on the NORTH | Prevent heat loss |
| Solar chimney | Ventilation |
| Specially designed SOLARIUM on the south | Heat gain |
| Windows + LIGHT SHELVES | Effective daylight distribution |
| Solar water heating + solar PV system | Active renewable energy |

## Himachal Pradesh State Co-operative Bank, Mall Road, Shimla
- Innovative combination of passive and active solar systems for a DAY-USE building to cut winter heating needs. Architect Ashok B. Lall; local architect C. L. Gupta; energy consultant S. S. Chandel. 1995–98. Built-up area 1650 m² (about 35% heated by the solar air-heating system); 38 m² of solar air-heating panels.
- External walls 23 cm masonry + 5 cm glass-wool insulation; ~155 m² double-glazed windows; corrugated GI roofing.
- Passive measures raised the cost from Rs 12,666/m² to Rs 13,346/m² — only 5.6% more (designed in from the start, compare with Himurja's 18.6%).
- Features: SUNSPACES on the south; SOLAR WALL on the south; rooftop SOLAR AIR-HEATING collector with ducts to the rooms (electrical back-up 3 × 15 kW, 4000 cfm blower); double-glazed windows; AIR-LOCK LOBBY at the main entrance (stops warm air escaping when the door opens).

| ✎ Exam tip: Use the two Shimla buildings to show "retrofit cost more (18.6%) vs designed-in cost less (5.6%)" — a strong argument for sustainable design from day one. |

# 5.3 Composite climate — Gurgaon and Delhi case studies

## RETREAT — Resource Efficient TERI Retreat for Environmental Awareness and Training, Gurgaon
- "A powerful and effective combination of modern science and traditional knowledge." 30-room training hostel with conference facilities; architects Sanjay Prakash and TERI; 1997–2000; 3000 m².
- Features: wall and roof INSULATION; oriented to maximise winter gains, summer gains offset by SHADING; east and west walls WITHOUT openings and shaded; EARTH-AIR TUNNELS — four tunnels, 70 m long, 70 cm diameter, 4 m below ground, supply conditioned air (earth stays at a steady temperature); four 2-hp fans push air in, SOLAR CHIMNEYS pull it out; ammonia ABSORPTION chillers for the conference block; HYBRID power — 50 kW biomass gasifier + 10.7 kWp solar PV with battery back-up; 2000 litres/day building-integrated solar water heating; CFLs and efficient tubes; daylighting and lighting controls; wastewater treated by a ROOT-ZONE system; building monitoring and management system.

## Solar Energy Centre, Gual Pahari, Gurgaon
- Demonstration of passive and active solar systems and innovative fenestration for thermal and visual comfort. Architect Vinod Gupta; 1984–90; 200-acre site; 6943 m².
- Technical & admin block: COURTYARD planning with single-loaded corridors for ventilation; landscaped courtyard modifies MICROCLIMATE; hollow concrete block walls reduce heat gain; designed windows and shading; rooftop EVAPORATIVE cooling; insulation for air-conditioned blocks.
- Workshop: section designed for ventilation and daylighting; roof heat gain minimised by insulation and REFLECTIVE finishes.
- Guest house: built on a south slope, partially EARTH-BERMED on three sides; summer-watered TERRACE GARDEN (evaporation cools); manually driven roof ventilators for cross-ventilation; WHITE reflective external paint; ARCHED SUNSHADES sized to block summer sun; solar water heaters for each bathroom; sunny terrace for SOLAR COOKING.

## Transport Corporation of India Ltd, Gurgaon
- Office with a climate-responsive built environment that uses the seasons to cut energy. A. B. Lall Architects; 1998/99; 2750 m².
- Features: inward-looking COMPACT form with controlled exposure; two window types — small PEEP windows (cross-ventilation and view) and windows for daylighting; peep-window REVEALS cut summer sun and admit winter sun; structural frames on transparent courts carry shading screens; LANDSCAPING as climate modifier; FOUNTAIN COURT with water columns; adjustable Venetian blinds inside double windows; polyurethane insulation on walls and roof; eco-friendly ABSORPTION air-conditioning; AC standards set by what office staff accept (not international norms); efficient lighting with daylight controls; reduced EMBODIED energy through less energy-intensive materials.

## Redevelopment of property at Civil Lines, Delhi (residential)
- Passive devices + innovative construction in a dense setting. Architect Ashok B. Lall; 1999; 1687 m².
- Features: ORIENTATION cuts summer sun, admits winter sun; sections let winter sun into north-side first-floor rooms; terraces with SKYLIGHTS; insulated sandwich walls; sunshading; COURTYARD design — the courtyard roof acts as a large EVAPORATIVE COOLER for the central space, with all rooms opening onto it; roof finished with China mosaic (white, reflective) over 30 mm polyurethane insulation; mosquito-proof evaporative cooler on the roof; double-rebated windows; WIND-DRIVEN evaporative cooler in the west house.

**Example:** The earth-air tunnel works like a cave — 4 m underground the soil stays at roughly the annual average temperature (~24–26 °C in Delhi), so air passing through is cooled in summer and warmed in winter.

# 5.4 Warm and humid climate — WBREDA office, Kolkata
- Office building of the West Bengal Renewable Energy Development Agency — showcases passive solar principles for warm-humid climate; well-lit and naturally ventilated all year; 25 kWp grid-interactive solar PV. Architect Gherzi Eastern Ltd; energy consultant TERI; 2000; plot 10,895 m², built-up 2026 m².

# UNIT 6 — CRADLE TO CRADLE THINKING

# 6.1 The problem: Industrial Revolution and "cradle to grave"
- McDonough and Braungart (Cradle to Cradle) describe the industrial system as if someone had been asked to "design a system of production that":

1. puts billions of pounds of TOXIC material into the air, water and soil every year;

2. produces materials so DANGEROUS they need constant vigilance by future generations;

3. results in GIGANTIC amounts of WASTE;

4. puts valuable materials in HOLES all over the planet (landfills) where they can never be retrieved;

5. needs thousands of complex REGULATIONS — not to keep people safe, but to keep them from being poisoned TOO QUICKLY;

6. measures productivity by how FEW people are working;

7. creates prosperity by digging up or cutting down natural resources and then BURYING or BURNING them;

8. ERODES the diversity of species and cultural practices.
- The LINEAR model: TAKE (destroy resources) → MAKE → WASTE = "FROM CRADLE TO GRAVE".
- ONE SIZE FITS ALL: industry designs one universal product/building for everyone, ignoring local climate, culture and resources (cartoon: an elephant, a bird and a turtle at the "one-size-fits-all" store).
- Contrast: VERNACULAR buildings that fit their place — igloo (snow), cave/earth houses (desert), thatched huts, houseboats — versus the same glass curtain-wall tower built everywhere regardless of climate.

**Example:** A glass office tower in Chennai needs huge air-conditioning because its design came from a cold country; a traditional Kerala house with deep verandahs and sloping tiled roofs stays cool naturally.

# 6.3 Eco-efficiency vs eco-effectiveness

## Eco-efficiency = "be LESS BAD"
- The industrial response was to redesign the system to: release FEWER toxic wastes; measure prosperity by LESS activity; MEET thousands of regulations; produce FEWER dangerous materials; result in SMALLER amounts of waste; put SMALLER amounts of valuable materials in holes.
- Problem: being "less bad" only SLOWS damage; it does not stop it. Recycling often means "DOWNCYCLING" — materials lose quality each round.

## Eco-effectiveness = "be MORE GOOD" (Cradle to Cradle)
- Design products and systems that are positively beneficial — USEFULNESS, CONVENIENCE and AESTHETICS together.
- Materials are designed from the start to go back safely into nature (biological nutrients) or back into industry at the same quality (technical nutrients).
- Example from the slides: the book "Cradle to Cradle" was printed by Melcher Media on a waterproof synthetic "paper" (like their "The Soothing Soak — a bathtub reader, entirely waterproof") that can be upcycled — the book itself is an example of its idea. (Contrast: a paper book or an e-reader.)

| Eco-efficiency | Eco-effectiveness |
| Do more with less | Do the right thing |
| Reduce harm ("less bad") | Create benefit ("more good") |
| Linear system made slower | Circular system — waste = food |
| Downcycling | Upcycling |

| ✎ Exam tip: A table comparing eco-efficiency and eco-effectiveness + the 8-point "retroactive design assignment" is an excellent long answer. |

# 6.4 Learning from nature — biophilia, growth and upcycling
- Benefits of TREES / green infrastructure: shade & heat protection, mental-health improvement, better air quality, stormwater management & water quality, energy savings, boost to the economy.
- HERMAN MILLER's office space design — daylight, plants and open, flexible workspaces (biophilic office).
- BIOPHILIA: (1) a human tendency to interact or be closely associated with other forms of life in nature; (2) a desire to commune with nature. Designing with plants, daylight, natural materials and views improves wellbeing.
- WHAT IS GROWTH? "Growth for the sake of growth is the ideology of the cancer cell" — Edward Abbey. Good growth is healthy growth, like nature's (a seedling, a tree) — not endless consumption.
- What can LEAF-CUTTER ANTS teach us about farming? They grow fungus on cut leaves — a closed loop where nothing is wasted.
- Modern box-type house vs traditional sloped-roof verandah house — local wisdom suits local climate. Contemporary Indian homes reinterpret this (bamboo screens, green balconies, courtyards).
- UPCYCLING — giving waste a new, higher-value life: e.g., a home built from colourful SHIPPING CONTAINERS decorated with old bicycle wheels.

# 6.5 The earth as a closed system — biological and technical cycles
- EARTH IS A CLOSED DYNAMIC SYSTEM: almost no matter enters or leaves; materials circulate (e.g., the water cycle: evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection).
- This works because of interactions among its FOUR SPHERES:

| Sphere | What it is |
| ATMOSPHERE | Air |
| GEOSPHERE (Lithosphere) | Rock, crust and mantle |
| HYDROSPHERE | Water |
| BIOSPHERE | The realm of all living things |

- Energy flows in (solar radiation) and out (terrestrial radiation); inside, temperature gradients drive motion, hydrologic cycling, geochemical cycling and biotic activity; HUMAN ACTIVITY sits at the centre and affects all of them.

## Two material cycles (Cradle to Cradle)

| BIOLOGICAL CYCLE — products for CONSUMPTION | TECHNICAL CYCLE — products for SERVICE |
| Plants → production → product → use phase → BIO-DEGRADATION → biological nutrients → plants | Production → product → use phase → RETURN & DISASSEMBLY → technical nutrients → production |
| Materials safely return to soil (food, natural fibres, compostable packaging) | Materials (metals, polymers) circulate in industry at high quality forever (TV, computer, car) |

- CIRCULAR ECONOMY (Ellen MacArthur Foundation diagram): biological nutrients cascade (biochemical feedstock, anaerobic digestion/composting, biogas, restoration); technical nutrients loop through MAINTENANCE → REUSE/REDISTRIBUTE → REFURBISH/REMANUFACTURE → RECYCLE; energy recovery and landfill ("leakage") minimised.

**Example:** A cotton T-shirt dyed with safe dyes can compost (biological cycle); a washing machine leased by the maker, returned and remanufactured follows the technical cycle.

| ✎ Exam tip: Draw both cycles side by side — this is the core of Cradle to Cradle. |

# UNIT 7 — NATURE INSPIRED DESIGN (NID)

# 7.1 NID principles

NATURE INSPIRED DESIGN uses nature as a model, measure and mentor. Six principles (life's principles):

| Principle | Meaning | Example |
| 1. WASTE EQUALS FOOD | Every output becomes an input for something else | Leaves fall and feed the soil; factory waste heat used by a neighbour |
| 2. USE (CURRENT) SOLAR INCOME / renewable energy | Run on today's sunlight, not stored fossil sunlight | Solar-powered products, daylighting |
| 3. BE RESOURCE EFFICIENT | Use minimum material and energy; shape over material | Honeycomb structures, hollow bones |
| 4. BE LOCALLY ATTUNED AND RESPONSIVE | Use local materials, energy and conditions | Vernacular architecture |
| 5. ADAPT AND EVOLVE TO CHANGING CONDITIONS | Be resilient, modular, upgradable | Adjustable façades, modular products |
| 6. INTEGRATE DEVELOPMENT WITH GROWTH | Grow in a way that builds up the system | Trees grow and strengthen their ecosystem |

- Shown as a hexagon diagram around "NID", and illustrated with a tree (roots, fungi, leaves, decay) and a building façade of adjustable timber slats.

# 7.3 The NID Wheel — tools for nature-inspired design
- The NID WHEEL arranges design tools in 8 segments around the product, linking design to biology, engineering, chemistry, ecology, business, market and medicine: VALUE, SALECYCLE, CONCEPT, WORKINGS, RECYCLING, HEALTH, FOODWEB, SYMBIOSIS.

| Segment | What the tools help you do | Example on the slides |
| VALUE | Determine specifications on which the product can compete; identify value-adding sustainability CERTIFICATES; create business models for closed- or open-loop PRODUCT-SERVICE SYSTEMS | Comparison charts of e-bikes/scooters — top speed, acceleration, range, weight, power, price |
| SALECYCLE | Create RECURRING revenues and customer benefits through extended product SERVICES; gear the same basic product to different customer segments and users | Circular-economy diagram; a child bike seat adapted for different users |
| CONCEPT | Fit into the mesh of PEOPLE, PRODUCTS and PLACES the product meets during use (content inventory table: buying, transport, installation, typical use, end-of-use, changes over time); find the underlying FUNCTIONS of the product to prepare for AskNature (searching nature for strategies); create positive impacts on the context of use | Product life map: stock → distribute → sale → experienced use → discard, with CO2/NOx and resource use at each step |
| WORKINGS | Explore specific strategies in nature for fulfilling the product's functions; discover the underlying working principles; use smart construction strategies from nature (useful for LIGHTWEIGHT design) | (A) hygroscopic surfaces collect water from moist air (desert beetle); (B) a capillary network transports water, absorbs heat and releases it by evaporation (thorny lizard); (C) heat released at specialised hot spots (elephant) |
| RECYCLING | Map LOCALLY AVAILABLE materials to find recycled resources; outline the activities needed to CLOSE material loops; ensure products can be optimally DISASSEMBLED for upgrading, repair, reuse and recycling | Herman Miller chair laid out in its many separable parts (design for disassembly) |
| HEALTH | Find where to improve the product system's efficiency for the largest ECO-EFFICIENCY gain; identify sub-optimal materials and prioritise replacements; find EVIDENCE of the product's positive impact | DESSO AirMaster carpet — lab test shows it traps fine dust (PM10) far better than a hard floor or standard carpet → healthier indoor air |
| FOODWEB | Find, list, select and estimate the size of OPPORTUNITIES in the product's direct surroundings; find the structure and main actors of the ECOLOGY around the product system | Food-web diagram (gull, fish, crabs, birds, plants, decomposers) used as a model for business ecosystems |
| SYMBIOSIS | Identify and involve the right people to develop an INDUSTRIAL SYMBIOSIS (one company's waste/by-product is another's resource) | BLOCK concrete safety barrier being crash-tested |

## More NID examples

| Example | Nature lesson |
| Colorwave 650 wide-format printer — cartridge redesigned using a REUSABLE carton | Waste equals food / resource efficiency |
| Philips Design — a responsive relaxation space inspired by nature (soft green light, glowing orbs) for stress-free rest | Biophilia, responsiveness |
| FESTO Bionicopter — a drone that REPELS birds at airfields the way a predator bird scares them away | Learning behaviour from nature (biomimicry of function) |

- Closing quote: "Civilization is always a race between LEARNING and DISASTER" — H. G. Wells (shown over a night-time satellite image of the lit-up earth).

# QUICK REVISION — one-line answers
- Sustainable design: minimise environmental harm, cut carbon, increase health, biodiversity and social value over the whole lifecycle.
- 1973 oil crisis → start of non-conventional energy thinking.
- Energy classification: usability (primary/secondary), traditional use (conventional/non-conventional), availability (renewable/non-renewable), commercial/non-commercial, origin.
- Non-conventional: free, clean, inexhaustible, quick — but dilute, costly to harness, uncontrollable, hard to transport/store.
- Greenhouse gases: CO2, CH4, N2O, HFCs, CFCs, HCFCs, SF6, O3.
- 6 principles: site, energy, water, space & material, indoor quality, operation & maintenance.
- Photoelectric effect (Einstein) → PN-junction solar cell (~0.5–0.6 V) → module: glass/EVA/cells/EVA/Tedlar.
- Solar thermal: water heater, Trombe wall, solar chimney, rock-bed air heating, absorption cooling, box & dish cookers.
- Wind: uneven solar heating + rotation; gradient height ~2000 m; surface layer ~100 m; P ∝ V³; cut-in, rated, furling speeds.
- Hybrid wind–PV: two sources, one battery via charge controller.
- Climate design: cold → gain heat; composite → block summer sun, admit winter sun; warm-humid → shade and ventilate.
- Cradle to grave (take–make–waste) vs cradle to cradle (waste = food).
- Eco-efficiency = less bad; eco-effectiveness = more good.
- Biological cycle (consumption, compost) vs technical cycle (service, disassembly, remanufacture).
- NID principles: waste = food, solar income, resource efficient, locally attuned, adapt & evolve, integrate development with growth.
- NID wheel: value, salecycle, concept, workings, recycling, health, foodweb, symbiosis.
