# 1.2 History of Design Management

DM sits between two worlds: DESIGN → DESIGN MANAGEMENT ← MANAGEMENT.

| Period | What happened |
| 1920s | The term "design management" was already used, but loosely defined. |
| 1950s | The Olivetti team explained DM as the reason for Olivetti's success. Business and design professionals met at the Aspen conference (USA). |
| 1970s | Peter Gorb (London Business School) argued designers should be trained in business. DM focused on defining design as a BUSINESS FUNCTION. |
| 1975 | Design Management Institute (DMI) set up in Boston with Harvard Business School. |
| 1980s | New schools/programmes; design schools offered DM specialisation with management schools. |
| 1990s | Strategy, policy, collaboration — "consumer is king". |
| 2000s | Profit by design, change by design, design-led technology, CSR, carbon footprint, ROI. |
| 2015 | Design thinking, startups, innovation, systems design. |
| 2025 | Design knowledge management, design-led innovation, design value creation, design-led development (not R&D but R&DD — research, design & development). |
| 2030 | Creative design-tech-led innovations. |

- In India, designers are often TOLD by management what to work on. In the world, corporates want designers to DIRECT others.
- Speculating scientifically and FORECASTING design trends is a design manager's task.
- DMI gives the Design Value Awards (2026) to organisations that show the value of design.

# 1.3 Definitions

## Design
- There are as many definitions as there are designers. Short version: "Creating a better novel future."
- For other professionals, creation looks invisible and mysterious. For DM, design must be seen as "a PROCESS" that can be DESCRIBED and DEMONSTRATED (otherwise it cannot be managed).

## Management
- "The art of getting things done." (Prof. PY adds jokingly: also "the science of how not to do them".)
- Formal: "The process of PLANNING, ORGANISING, LEADING and MONITORING the work of organisational members and of using all available organisational resources to reach stated organisational goals."
- Management = deciding for tomorrow, today — through vision, strategy and tactics.

## Design Management
- "Design management is an INVESTMENT that transforms CREATIVITY into REALITY, delivering better outcomes for all involved."
- For a design MANAGER: managing for design means EMPOWERING co-designers and co-managers with knowledge, tools and processes, so that through design they become key contributors to the organisation's growth.
- For the designer inside the manager (PY): DM is managing design-induced processes to achieve 100% transformation of the NEEDS, WANTS and DESIRES of users, whose satisfaction leads to PROFITABLE business.
- It means taking ideas through a series of stages towards implementation, within a predefined vision.
- Company's view: idea → product launch takes years and investment, so every decision (including the designer's) must be right at every stage. GOOD DESIGN DOES NOT HAPPEN BY ITSELF — IT HAS TO BE MANAGED.

| ✎ Exam tip: Quote "Good design does not happen by itself; it has to be managed" and the PY definition (needs, wants, desires → satisfaction → profitable business). |

# 1.4 Design as a strategic ASSET
- Design is a process, a method, a way of thinking, doing and deciding — and a profession.
- But it is also a unique RESOURCE: accumulated wealth, intellectual property, knowledge equity (like a trade secret).
- "Design is SPICE and MEDICINE for innovation" — too much kills, too little is ineffective, the right dose keeps it healthy.
- Strategic question: how can this wealth be multiplied and spread across corporations, markets, society and the nation?
- India's creative industry is about $30 billion and employs about 8% of the working population; creative exports grew ~20% to over $11 billion.
- Strategic design thinking builds a new mental model, new forms, a visionary desired future state, and turns challenges into opportunities and strategic objectives.

# 1.5 Attributes of successful design leaders

A good design leader can:
- Articulate and DEFEND design without dominating others.
- BRIDGE the gap between visionary ideas and real-world practice.
- Align design decisions with USER DATA and show ROI.
- Earn more weight for the "design voice" (the voice of users) in meetings.
- Grow self and team; set clear steps from idea to launch.
- ANTICIPATE disaster outcomes and keep alternatives ready ("design is magic").
- Empower other experts to stick to timelines, budgets and tools.
- Connect design vision to company MISSION and GOALS.
- DELEGATE according to people's capabilities; share limelight and rewards.
- Ask pointed, clear questions; bend without breaking backs; make others feel they OWN the ideas.
- Practise common sense — a sensible, sensitive, insightful success story.
- 2026 addition: anticipate and harness technology (AI) — not just control or copy it, but make it work for humanity.

**Example:** In a college fest team, the design lead who lets the poster team own their idea, keeps a backup printer ready, and shows the sponsor how the new layout increased footfall is showing these attributes.

# 1.6 Typical managerial tasks of a design manager (IMPORTANT)

| Task | Meaning | Watch-outs / tools |
| 1. PLANNING | Think through goals and actions in advance, based on method/logic, not hunches | Pitfall: hasty plan on incomplete information; not thinking of implications |
| 2. ORGANISING | Coordinate human, material and environmental resources towards the design goal | Error-free communication; are resources available?; hierarchy of responsibility |
| 3. LEADING | Show the way; direct and influence processes towards the outcome; predict success, prevent failure | Clear idea of time; whom to contact |
| 4. IMPLEMENTING | Execute the plan to its logical end — "birthing and delivery" of the design | Checklists, feedback loops |
| 5. CONTROLLING | Monitor performance parameters so that good design emerges | Time-based reports, progress reports; what if lagging? who is affected by delay? |
| 6. DE-BRIEFING | Follow up, document and learn from experience | Completion report, bills/filing, review & conclusion meetings, documentation |

**Example (college fest):** Plan the stage design → organise carpenters, lights, volunteers → lead the team on the day → implement the set → control timing and budget → debrief: what went wrong with the sound check, written down for next year.

# 1.7 Questions DM answers, and how a manager is judged

## Questions DM helps answer
- How to articulate the VALUE of a design solution (metrics)?
- How to get recognition, credibility and trust for design in business, industry, education and the public?
- How to build viable relationships inside the organisation for design goals?
- How to set up efficient processes that give good output every time?
- An individual action plan to improve your own position and the design function's position in the hierarchy.
- How to MONETISE design value — a creative, circular design economy; how to profit from creativity.

## A design manager's expertise is judged by how they manage
- Relationships between the organisation and public/stakeholders through design outcomes.
- Relationships between the organisation and consumers — loyalty, trust, satisfaction, fans.
- Teams within the organisation, and self as a professional.
- Processes and procedures (design project methodology).
- Linking the organisation's information systems to deliver customer experience through design KNOWLEDGE (not just skills).
- Making the organisation FUTURE-SHOCK PROOF — design forecasting based on analytics.

# UNIT 2 — ORGANISATIONS, CORE COMPETENCE, VISION & MISSION (1 Sep)

# 2.1 What is an organisation?
- An ORGANISATION is an entity where a group of people sharing interests come together for a PURPOSE and PRODUCE together.
- Designers must understand how organisations are built to become commercial successes.
- Organisations are like humans: they must GROW to stay alive; if they stop growing they die. Life cycle: infancy → adolescence → maturity → middle age → decline.
- Growth must be planned, orderly, controlled and directed; it needs inputs; design is a powerful VALUE ENHANCER; managers must be good at managing growth.
- Organisations need a STRATEGY for growth, including facing competition. The competitive edge comes from CORE COMPETENCIES.
- Types: company, industry, corporate, NGO, government body, institution, private firm, society, guild, club, professional body, startup.
- This century, DESIGN is increasingly becoming a core competency and an impetus for growth.

# 2.2 Core competency
- Core competency = the ability of an organisation to be ESPECIALLY GOOD at a particular activity. Concept by C.K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel.
- Made of the sum of individual skills and experience harnessed to resources (may include information and technology).

## Three tests of a core competency

1. NOT EASY for competitors to IMITATE.

2. REUSABLE widely across many products and markets.

3. CONTRIBUTES to the end consumer's experienced benefits and value.

## Examples
- Tata: low-cost, high-efficiency manufacturing and design + brand trust + leadership.
- Apple: design and mastery of hardware–software integration. Pillars: hardware-software integration, design & UX, ecosystem, supply chain, brand equity & marketing. Rivals can copy one piece but not the whole.
- IIT Guwahati DoD: designers who are also technocrats; UE-UI-UX specialisation 2003–2015; pioneers of UI/UX/IxD in India; B.Des placements better than CS at times.
- Class quiz: what is the core competency of DoD IIITDM Jabalpur?
- If you start a startup, make DESIGN its core competency. "The organisation of the future will make design a core competency."
- Competency prepares you for competition. Management's role is to ESTABLISH core competences — they are the central building blocks.

| ⚠ Note: Design is often treated as COSMETICS (aesthetics, GUIs, apps) — this undersells it. Design can do much more. |

| ✎ Exam tip: Define core competency (Prahalad & Hamel) + the 3 tests + one example (Apple or Tata). This is a classic short question. |

# 2.4 Vision, Goals, Mission, Purpose

## VISION
- The ASPIRATION of the organisation — what the founders dreamt, adopted as the organisation's aspiration.
- Example: Nehru called the IITs "Temples of Learning".
- It reflects the eventual grown organisation and its success — a statement of FUTURE status and how it will get there.

## GOALS
- A desired state of affairs to be reached, set in advance.
- A desire becomes a goal ONLY if an action to achieve it exists and is activated (doing).
- In design: talk less, demonstrate more — your design is your statement of the goal reached.
- Goal management (controlling): recognise the goals of team members, abandon irrelevant goals, identify and resolve conflicts, prioritise.

## MISSION ∩ VISION = PURPOSE

| Vision | Mission |
| Inside → out (push) | Outside → in (pull of life) |
| Feeling, passion | Sensing, zeal, accomplishing |

- Where they overlap is PURPOSE — the reason the organisation exists beyond commerce. Anyone can make profit; the question is HOW and WHAT guides the path.
- Mission answers "why do we exist?"; Vision answers "what do we want to be?"
- A startup can start envisioning anywhere in the middle — e.g., start with goals, then find the inner vision, then objectives and strategy.

**Example:** Zomato — Vision: "better food for more people"; Mission: what it does daily (connect people with restaurants, deliver fast); Goal: 10-minute delivery in metro cities by a date.

# UNIT 3 — STRATEGY (1–2 Sep)

# 3.1 What is strategy?
- STRATEGY = a plan of action (thinking) designed to achieve a particular goal; the motivation is "winning" (ideally win-win). It involves choosing from many options.
- "A strategy is an idea that distinguishes a course of action by its hypothesis that a certain FUTURE POSITION offers an ADVANTAGE for acquiring some designated gain."
- Originally a military term. Game theory is used in organisational strategy and by designers in design thinking.
- Strategy MANTRA: Where are you now? Where do you want to go? How will you get there? (There is always more than one way.)
- Strategy is highly CONTEXTUAL and can change. Changing it TOO OFTEN shows incompetent/confused leadership or a copied strategy (e.g., CEO resignations in aviation and AI firms).
- An organisation's strategy is a CONFIDENTIAL road map to its mission and objectives — matching resources and capabilities to the external environment, with risk. Competency reduces risk.
- Strategy is communicated through POLICY DOCUMENTS (e.g., India's National Design Policy); the strategy itself stays confidential but its "description" is shared with teams.
- Objectives describe the vision-mission (why adopted, with what aim and purpose).

# 3.2 Strategy vs Tactics
- TACTICS = a mini-strategy at day-to-day level; the immediate method to achieve a goal with resources at hand. Greek "taktike" = art of organising an army. Indian parallel: Chanakya niti.

| Strategy | Tactics |
| Planning (thinking) | Doing |
| Large scale | Smaller scale |
| WHY | HOW |
| Difficult to copy | Easy to copy |
| Long time frame; needs time to form | Short time frame; quick, reactionary decisions |

- One can become the other depending on context.

**Example:** Football — the coach's strategy is "keep possession and attack from the wings"; a player's tactic is dodging a defender and passing at that moment.

# 3.3 Strategy pyramids

## Wendy McGuinness strategy pyramid

| Level | Element | Question |
| PURPOSE | Mission / Values / Vision | Why do we exist? What is important to us? What do we want to be? |
| STRATEGY | Strategic intent / Drivers / Enablers | How will we get there? What forces are at work? What frameworks, resources, skills will we use? |
| EXECUTION | Targets & initiatives / Performance indicators / Strategy map | What must we do? How will we know we succeed? How will we test and communicate? |

## Eric Partaker's pyramid of strategy (top to bottom)

1. Vision — WHY: the ultimate destination.

2. Mission — WHAT you do, whom you serve, your impact.

3. Core values — HOW: guiding principles.

4. Strategic priorities — 2–5 big bets.

5. Long-term goals — 3–5 year milestones.

6. Initiatives — programmes/projects.

7. Strategies — the how-to playbook.

8. Tactics — concrete actions and tools.

9. Action plan — day-to-day steps with timelines, resources, responsibilities.

# 3.4 Strategic planning process (the funnel)

Inputs (personal values, history, employee ideas, trends, organisational values) → Mission → Market → Key success factors → SWOT → Assumptions → Ideal → Key result areas → Objectives → Strategies → Action plans (who, what, when).
- Objectives should be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Aggressive (ambitious), Realistic, Time-based.
- PEST variables (political, economic, social, technological) keep changing and force tactical changes.

# 3.5 Levels of strategy

| Level | Concern | Example question |
| BUSINESS strategy | How a single firm/unit COMPETES in its chosen arena (outside world: customers, competitors) | How will our shoe brand beat Bata in tier-2 cities? |
| CORPORATE strategy | The overall diversified firm: WHICH businesses to be in, and how being in one helps the others (internal world: administration, resources, innovation culture) | Should Tata enter electric scooters? |
| DESIGN strategy | How to use design as a resourceful ASSET (both inside and outside) | New product line? Rebrand existing products? Import a part from outside? |
| NETWORK strategy | Partners and alliances | Which suppliers/partners to tie up with? |

# 3.6 Business strategy analysis checklist (6 steps)

1. BUSINESS CONTEXT — market conditions, industry trends, competitive landscape; goals & objectives; strengths, weaknesses, opportunities.

2. CUSTOMER ANALYSIS — segments, needs, preferences, behaviour; satisfaction & feedback; acquisition & retention.

3. COMPETITIVE ANALYSIS — direct and indirect competitors, positioning; their strengths, weaknesses, strategies, market share; differentiation opportunities.

4. FINANCIAL ANALYSIS — income statement, balance sheet, cash flow; profitability, revenue growth, cost structure vs benchmarks.

5. MARKET ANALYSIS — segmentation, size & growth; trends, regulation, technology; niche markets.

6. STRATEGIC FIT — alignment with capabilities; uses core competencies; feasible and scalable.

# 3.7 How to plan a DESIGN STRATEGY — 9 steps (IMPORTANT)

1. Study the core company / corporate strategy.

2. Study the marketing, financial and production/manufacturing strategies.

3. Analyse the present product portfolio — INTERNAL design audit.

4. Do SWOT with respect to competition — EXTERNAL design audit.

5. Project future scenarios using PREDICTIVE ANALYTICS.

6. Prepare a plan for embedding company DESIGN POLICY (studio / functional level).

7. Plan for implementing designs (OPERATIONAL level).

8. Implement designs (EXECUTION level).

9. Design DE-BRIEFING with documentation.
- Design strategy sits at the centre of INTERNAL factors (client, business strategy, brand) and EXTERNAL factors (society, market, technology).
- The design manager's job is easier when the whole organisation is sensitive to the value of design (a design-thinking culture).

**Example:** A kitchen-appliance company: study its "affordable premium" corporate strategy → check marketing/production plans → audit its own mixers (outdated look) → SWOT vs Philips/Bajaj → forecast demand for compact appliances → set a design language policy → plan the new range → launch → debrief sales and user feedback.

| ✎ Exam tip: The 9 steps are often asked directly. Memory hook: "Study, Study, Audit-in, Audit-out, Predict, Policy, Plan, Implement, Debrief." |

# 3.8 Blue Ocean Strategy
- By W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne (2005). Move away from ultra-competitive battles on price and features (RED OCEAN) into a NEW market where competition is absent or irrelevant (BLUE OCEAN).
- Focus is VALUE INNOVATION. DESIGN is the antidote and vaccine for red oceans.

| Red Ocean | Blue Ocean |
| Compete in EXISTING market space | CREATE uncontested market space |
| BEAT the competition | Make competition IRRELEVANT |
| Exploit existing demand | Create and capture NEW demand |
| Make the value–cost trade-off | BREAK the value–cost trade-off (differentiation AND low cost) |
| Intense competition, margin erosion, incremental thinking | Value reinvention, superior profits |
| e.g., coffee chains in the same area fighting with discounts | e.g., Airbnb created a new travel category |

- Like football: instead of tackling head-on, dodge and pass into open space.

# 3.9 Design strategy variables at PRODUCT level

## Operational planning questions
- How do we get teamwork into product planning? How do we coordinate marketing and production?
- How long will present products stay profitable? What future markets?
- What product changes will competition force? What IPR/government policy issues? What technology innovations are possible?

## User-trend tracking radar
- Macro lifestyle trends, fashion trends & forecasts, customer taste, media trends (digital, TV, ads); separate temporary FADS from long-term SHIFTS.

## Related marketing & production strategies
- Product line and product mix combinations, product repositioning, changing the marketing mix, refocusing advertising.
- PRODUCT LINE = a group of related products from one company. PRODUCT MIX = all product lines together (width = number of lines; depth = variants in a line).

| Milk products | Yoghurt products | Butter products |
| Full fat | Plain | Salted |
| Semi-skimmed | Vanilla | Unsalted |
| Skimmed | Fruit | Garlic |

- MARKETING MIX — 4Ps: PRODUCT, PRICE, PLACE, PROMOTION. The goal: the right product at the right price, at the right place, with the right promotion.

**Example:** Amul — product line "butter" (salted, unsalted, garlic); product mix = milk + butter + cheese + ice cream; priced low, sold in every kirana (place), promoted with the Amul girl topical ads.

# UNIT 4 — BUSINESS PLANNING, ENTREPRENEURSHIP & STARTUPS (3 Sep)

# 4.1 What is a plan?
- A PLAN is a proposed method of getting from one set of circumstances to another — from the present towards objectives. Planning means working out sub-components in detail; TIME matters.
- PLANNING = organising and making advance decisions on how to achieve goals: decide what to accomplish, make an action plan, allocate resources; be efficient and effective, set priorities, catch opportunities, avoid problems.
- A plan is a set of instructions that is DATA/FACT-DRIVEN, TIME-BOUND, with MEASURABLE objectives. If any of these is missing, it is NOT a plan.

| Level | Horizon | Microsoft example |
| Vision | 10–100 years | "A PC on every desk" |
| Strategy | 3–5 years | Make the Microsoft–PC link indispensable (the OS) |
| Plan (tactical) | 1 year | Push AI into the OS so it can't be used fully till you pay for the upgrade |

- CORPORATE planning = the organisation as a whole, long term. BUSINESS planning = what is done NOW for targets in a particular sector (tactical).

# 4.2 The business plan

## Elements

Executive summary, mission statement, company background, product description, marketing plan, competitor analysis, SWOT, operations, financial planning, timeline.

## Planning document structure

1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — one page (1–2 for small startups); written LAST, after the plan is finalised.

2. INTRODUCTION — industry and how you fit; purpose and objectives.

3. OPERATIONS PLAN — structure, location, regulations.

4. MARKETING PLAN.

5. MANAGEMENT PLAN.

6. FINANCIAL PLAN — financing, costing, projections.
- Review it regularly.

## Process of developing a business plan

Internal situation analysis → External situation analysis → SWOT → GAP analysis → Resources assessment → Scenarios / WHAT-IF analysis → Economic forecast → Budget & finance modelling.
- What-if scenarios shown VISUALLY are the DESIGNER'S contribution to business planning.

## Analysis tools
- SITUATION ANALYSIS: where you are, how you got there, what you have, what is missing. External: customers, competition, political environment — who is out there, what they do, trends and effects.
- PESTLE: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental.
- SWOT: Strengths & Weaknesses (internal: helpful/harmful), Opportunities & Threats (external).
- GAP ANALYSIS: a visual of where you are vs where you want to be (vs where you should have been).

## Lean business plan (one-page) template

Executive summary, problem statement, solution, target market, revenue streams, cost structure, marketing plan, key metrics, team, funding requirements, competitive analysis, risk assessment.
- A PROJECT PLAN works at programme level within the overall business plan; it manages and controls one project.

## Canvases
- MVP / Experiment canvas.
- PRODUCT–MARKET FIT canvas: Value proposition side (positioning, products & services, key features, key metrics) ↔ Customer segment side (characteristics, jobs-to-be-done, problems & needs, user experience).

**Example (PESTLE for an e-scooter startup):** Political — EV subsidies; Economic — fuel prices; Social — young commuters; Technological — battery costs; Legal — licence rules for low-speed scooters; Environmental — pollution concerns.

# 5.2 Product Life Cycle (PLC)
- Stages: INTRODUCTION → GROWTH → MATURITY → DECLINE → DEATH / ABANDONMENT (obsolescence).

| Stage | Sales | Profit / cash flow | What is happening |
| Introduction | Low, slow | NEGATIVE cash flow — development & production cost is high | Product launched, few buyers |
| Growth | Rising fast | Profit begins | Word spreads, competitors enter |
| Maturity | Peaks | Highest net revenue | Market saturated |
| Decline | Falling | Shrinks to loss | Newer products replace it |

- PROFIT peaks BEFORE SALES peak — the gap is the LAG. ROR (rate of return) slope shows how fast investment returns.

## Strategies to extend the PLC

1. Strategy 1 — BY DESIGN: INNOVATION RESTARTS the PLC (a new growth curve begins at maturity). One can also RENOVATE the existing product, or give a discount / lower the price.

2. Strategy 2 — Re-advertise as "improved", "imported", etc.

3. Strategy 3 — Buy out a competing brand.

4. Strategy 4 — Free service for life; discount sale.

5. Strategy 5 — Buy one get one free (to clear dead inventory, crowded shelves, dead investment).
- "Design by innovation / design thinking" is this decade's last business card to play a leadership role.
- PLC analysis is a full-time job in companies; product designers are increasingly taking this responsibility.

**Example:** Maggi — introduced 1983, grew, matured; extended by new flavours, atta noodles, cup noodles (innovation restarting the curve) and by re-advertising after the 2015 ban.

# 5.3 Business Analytics (BA)
- BA = skills, technologies and practices for continuous, iterative exploration of PAST business performance to gain insight and drive planning. It uses statistics, descriptive and predictive modelling and fact-based management.
- BA answers: Why is this happening? What if these trends continue? What will happen next (predict)? What is the best that can happen (optimise)?
- More questions: What do customers expect? Future sales of a service? Best and worst quality aspects of the product? Best suppliers and most profitable customers? Which marketing campaigns will attract the target audience?
- Designers aiming for corporate positions need extra qualifications (e.g., an online data-analytics degree from IIT Madras without an entrance exam).

## Four types of BA (Gartner — value increases with difficulty)

| Type | Question | Output |
| DESCRIPTIVE | What happened? (hindsight) | Real-time dashboards, email reports |
| DIAGNOSTIC | Why did it happen? | Analytic dashboard from past performance |
| PREDICTIVE | What will happen? (insight) | Predictive forecast of likely scenarios |
| PRESCRIPTIVE | How can we make it happen? (foresight / optimisation) | Rules and recommendations — the MOST VALUABLE |

**Example (food app):** Descriptive — orders fell 10% last month. Diagnostic — because delivery time rose in rain. Predictive — next monsoon orders will drop 15%. Prescriptive — add rain-time riders and a "rain surge" offer.

# 5.4 Data science and the designer's role
- Fields of data science: data analysis, data mining, machine learning, big data, software tools, methods & algorithms (from descriptive to prescriptive; experimental to theoretical).
- DATA SCIENCE = Mathematics expertise ∩ Technology/hacking skills ∩ Business/strategy acumen. Designers can easily master the BUSINESS component.
- The INTERPRETATION of analysis output is where designers become CRUCIAL. A designer need not be a maths expert — the ability to SPECIFY VARIABLES and SPOT PATTERNS OF BEHAVIOUR in data is a designer's unique aptitude.

## Data mining
- Systematic use of statistical and mathematical methods on large data to find new PATTERNS (pattern recognition).
- Flow: data collection → selection, exploration, manipulation → data / text / web mining → analysis → patterns → interpretation → findings.
- Applications: risk analysis, retargeting, affinity / market-basket analysis, customer clustering, competition analysis, customer profiling, target-group selection.
- This is a management AND designer responsibility; UX/UI/IxD designers increasingly gain this expertise using AI.

## Predictive analytics
- Detects unrecognised relationships and patterns in structured and unstructured data, plus external factors, to predict FUTURE EVENTS.
- Gathering (data) → Forecast (statistics, data mining, machine learning → information) → Action (knowledge).
- Designers need to equip themselves with STATISTICS.

## DIKW ladder

DATA → INFORMATION → SKILL → KNOWLEDGE → INSIGHT → WISDOM. "Analytics — the art of making better decisions and better choices."
- Data that impacts investment in innovation: data on USERS, COMPETITION, COMPANY HEALTH, COMPANY PERFORMANCE, POLITICS & POLICY.

## Tools and basics
- Analytical tools: SAS, R, statistical packages, SPSS. Automation/productivity: SQL, Excel, Excel dashboards, database management.
- Excel tricks designers can use: flash fill, pivot tables, goal seek, conditional formatting, INDEX & MATCH, waterfall charts, forecasting. Every design researcher (PhD) studies statistics and computing.
- Data structures: binary trees, stacks, matrix, trees, arrays, heap, linked list. Top 7 algorithms: sorting, searching, hashing, dynamic programming, binary exponentiation, string matching/parsing, primality testing. OUTCOMES are MODELS for predicting.

## Types of predictive models

| Model | Use |
| Linear models | Relationship between two or more variables (graphs) |
| Generalised additive models | Non-parametric regression |
| Logistic regression | Probability that a case belongs to a target class |
| Survival analysis | Reliability analysis (how long until failure) |
| Decision trees | Rules that progressively split the population into homogeneous groups |
| Bayesian methods | Classify using conditional event probabilities |

- Machine learning types: SUPERVISED, UNSUPERVISED, REINFORCEMENT (Python / R code). AI has made it a walk-through; a designer must know the basics — the hard work is done by AI.

# UNIT 6 — BRANDS & BRAND MANAGEMENT (7 Sep)

# 6.1 Origin and definition of "brand"
- Branding was NOT invented by advertising. The word comes from Old Norse "brandr" = TO BURN — farmers burnt identification marks on cattle. Egyptian tomb inscriptions (2000 BC) also used marks. Today: RFID tags on cattle.
- Modern brand grew from the NEED to draw the ATTENTION of a buyer to a product surrounded by competitors (brand as MARKETING TOOL).
- It became a powerful STRATEGIC WEAPON in market warfare (brand as strategic business tool). Branding needs professional DESIGN inputs beyond arts and graphics.
- AMA definition: "A name, term, sign, symbol or design, or a combination of them, intended to identify the goods or services of one seller or group of sellers and to DIFFERENTIATE them from those of competitors." Kotler & Armstrong: it identifies the MAKER or SELLER.

## Is a trademark, name or logo a brand? — NO
- Trademarks and logos CONTRIBUTE to the evolution of a brand, but individually they are NOT brands; they are IDENTITIES of a brand.
- A brand is an ABSTRACT entity. Brands differ from trademarks and other IPR in that they have NO EXPIRY DATE.

# 6.2 Six levels of meaning of a brand

For both owner and user, a brand has six levels of meaning (Mercedes-Benz example):

# 6.3 From BRANDS to BRANDING
- BRANDING = the act of creating a distinctive IMAGE in the COGNITIVE realm of users.
- A brand is a MENTAL CONSTRUCT, reflected through design elements that represent what it stands for. The design elements are NOT the brand themselves.
- Branding is a process, a tool, a strategy and an orientation of attitudes to ELICIT a desired BEHAVIOUR; a process by which a marketer builds LONG-TERM RELATIONSHIPS.
- MANAGING a brand is different from DESIGNING a brand.

| ⚠ Note: Mistaken understanding: designers often think graphics, colour, form, typography = brand/branding. By designing them they think they have created a brand. What gets created in the user's MENTAL COGNITIVE SPACE is the brand. |

# 6.5 Brand equity, image and personality

## BRAND EQUITY
- Equity = real value of an asset after debts and liabilities are subtracted. Brand equity = the total GOODWILL value of a brand. It keeps growing as the brand consolidates; it can be expressed as a number/value.

| Measure | Meaning |
| Brand AWARENESS | Number of people in a population who RECOGNISE the brand |
| Brand ACCEPTANCE | Number of aware people positively inclined (or with no negative attitude) |
| Brand PREFERENCE | Number who CHOOSE it over an equally good rival |
| Brand LOYALTY | Number who CONSISTENTLY buy it over a long period |
| Brand VALUE | Cost differential — how much MORE a user will pay vs an exactly similar competitor |

- Brand equity is used only as an INDEX of goodwill; it is NOT "brand valuation".

## BRAND IMAGE
- Ogilvy treated brand personality and brand image as the same.
- Brand image = the AURA, REPUTATION and SYMBOLIC MEANING attached to a brand in users' perception; the sum total MENTAL MODEL in consumers'/stakeholders' minds. Personality describes the properties of the branded item.
- Designers use IMAGE BOARDS (collages made by experts) to ideate this image. Quiz: create an image board for DoD, your startup or yourself.

## BRAND PERSONALITY
- The "persona" a brand gets associated with by evocation. Example: a preteen said "Dabur is like my grandfather" — that sums up brand personality.
- As brands age, they signify human traits; personality distinguishes brands in the SAME product line.
- Designing personality asks: "What happens when this brand becomes a human being?" Example: Liril soap's 1975 bold swimsuit ad that did not evoke angry reactions (fresh, free personality).
- In design this is studied under SEMANTICS (a vast research area).
- Do NOT confuse BRAND AMBASSADOR with brand personality — but a personality can become an ambassador (the Amul girl; the Nirma girl — named after Karsanbhai Patel's daughter Nirupama; door-to-door selling grew it into a household name).

# 7.2 What is creativity? — definitions
- Creativity = the ability to create. CREATE (Chambers 1989): to bring into being out of nothing, by force of imagination; to make, produce, form, design; to invest with a new form; to institute something new.
- For an artist it means something different from a scientist. For a DESIGNER it is a SKILL — a thinking activity that results in DESIGN CONCEPTS. It drives innovation through PROBLEM SOLVING. In art it can be an insight; in design, the insight MUST lead to a PRACTICAL solution.

| Author | Definition |
| Mednick (1964) | Framing new combinations of associative elements which either meet a specified requirement or are useful |
| — | Generating new original ideas |
| Barron (1961) | Not exploration, invention or discovery — it is making NEW COMBINATIONS out of the existing |
| Pradeep N. Khandwalla (1988) | Creativity is a STATE OF MIND |
| Hurlock (1978) | A personal achievement of uniqueness, not necessarily universal |

- All definitions are valid; creativity cannot be reduced to one. The OUTPUT can be seen, but the PROCESS behind it is deeply buried.
- The only way to grasp it fully is to PLAYFULLY INDULGE in it, experience it and understand it METACOGNITIVELY.
- Creativity is a phenomenon by which something NEW and VALUABLE is formed by the power of the mind through thinking. It may be tangible or intangible. It can be measured; it is a form of INTELLIGENCE.

# 7.3 How creative ideas appear
- All of us have creative moments — while solving puzzles, relaxing in a bath (72% of people get new ideas in the shower). Some recognise them; many don't.
- IDEAS are the outcome of creative thinking = IDEATION. Creativity drives innovation. The IDEATION stage of design thinking is fuelled by creativity.
- Ideas often appear spontaneously, as if in a FLASH, when the mind is engaged elsewhere (music, walking — a state of FLOW, meditation).
- Examples: KEKULÉ discovered the hexagonal BENZENE RING after dreaming of a snake eating its own tail. ARCHIMEDES found the law of BUOYANCY while taking a bath.
- The RELAXED SUBCONSCIOUS mind is involved. So creativity does NOT lend itself to "planning" — it can strike anywhere, any time. Carry a notepad / journal / sketchbook. One can TRAIN the mind to direct thoughts.

## Myths
- Creativity is often mistaken for academic/scholastic intelligence, memory or knowledge.
- Artists and designers are thought to be "crazy / weird" — but creativity is a basic human DNA trait present in ALL.
- Neuro-divergent people often think differently and are more inclined to creativity — different, not weird.

## Creativity as a thinking pattern
- Creativity = the ability to TRANSCEND traditional thinking and develop new ideas through ABDUCTIVE thought.
- It goes beyond imagining to SYNTHESISING: if it is an idea, you research/experiment to prove it; if a new process, you try and test it — it often produces NOVELTY. Creativity is a unique PATTERN of thinking.

## Left brain vs right brain

| Left brain | Right brain |
| Sequential thinking, logic, cause & effect | Insight, imagination |
| Language, grammar, speaks well | Face recognition, spatial orientation |
| Facts, good with numbers, word puzzles | Drama, metaphor, poetry, music, rhythm |
| Analyses, names things | Meditation, art, colours |

- Stroop test ("say the colour, not the word") shows left/right dominance. Designers need BOTH sides. Creative potential is curtailed by our education system and by ourselves.

# 7.4 Creativity process, innovation and mental blocks

## The creativity process in cognition

AWARENESS → INCUBATION → ILLUMINATION → VERIFICATION (= creativity) → IMPLEMENTATION → MARKETING & SELLING (= innovation).
- Kotler: innovation is the process of taking new ideas through to a SATISFIED CUSTOMER. The relationship between creativity and innovation is governed by COMMERCE.

**Example:** You notice students struggling with hostel laundry (awareness) → think on and off for days (incubation) → idea of a QR slot-booking system hits you while walking (illumination) → test with 20 users (verification) → build it (implementation) → sell to other hostels (marketing).

## MENTAL BLOCKS
- During a thinking marathon we suddenly get STUCK and keep returning to the same old idea — a MENTAL BLOCK.
- The brain is the laziest organ: it needs ~40 W and preserves energy for survival.
- Hundreds of techniques exist to unblock; this course covers two: BRAINSTORMING and SCAMPER.
- SCAMPER: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify/Magnify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange.

**Example (SCAMPER on an umbrella):** Substitute — fabric with transparent plastic; Combine — umbrella + torch; Adapt — car-door umbrella; Modify — reverse-folding so it doesn't drip; Put to other use — sunshade on a bike; Eliminate — the handle (hands-free hat umbrella); Reverse — inverted umbrella.

# 7.5 AI and creativity

## Tips for using AI in creative thinking (prompt engineering, from Stanford d.school)
- Don't ask AI questions — make AI ASK YOU questions.
- Treat AI as an advisor/teammate, not a slave doing your work.

# 7.6 Design Thinking (DT)
- Stanford d.school process: EMPATHISE → DEFINE → IDEATE → PROTOTYPE → TEST (4-, 5- and 6-step versions exist).

| ⚠ Note: Prof. PY: "Unfortunately this is not what actual DT is" — textbooks and management schools have made it a BUZZWORD. A better model: PROBLEM SPACE (understand, observe, point of view) → SOLUTION SPACE (ideate, prototype, test), with diverge–converge in each. |

## Characteristics of the DT method

DT is the ability to think like a designer (a "designerly" way of thinking) at each step — an open-minded problem-solving approach with:
- A HUMAN-CENTRED focus — the living being at the centre, starting with EMPATHY (not sympathy).
- A keen OBSERVING and QUESTIONING mind.
- CRITICAL THINKING — dig deep into unmet and unsaid needs; DEFINE the real problem.
- Cross-domain IDEATION by ABDUCTION of knowledge and practices.
- Hands-on PROTOTYPING — fail early to succeed sooner.
- Ability to formulate TESTS for validating design decisions.
- Empathise > Define > Ideate > Prototype > Test are the core characteristics — but WITH CREATIVITY. Most so-called experts stay silent on the creative part. DT is not taught as a separate course in design schools because the whole four-year curriculum IS DT.

## Design methods and thinking directions
- Design methods = Methods of THINKING (mental phenomena) + Methods of DOING (physical phenomena).
- DOUBLE DIAMOND (UK Design Council): DIVERGENT thinking (come up with many different/variant ideas) and CONVERGENT thinking (aim at the "correct" solution to the given problem). DT encourages diverging to ideate many solutions (possible or impossible), then converging to choose and realise the best. Also: whole-to-part and part-to-whole approaches. IDEO model; DT infinity loop.
- Design thinking = diverge then converge; futures thinking = mostly diverge (exploring possible futures).

## DT cannot guarantee success
- DT processes do not necessarily yield results; DT is an AID to designing. Simply following the stages does not mean a usable design solution.
- IDEA vs CONCEPT: an idea is NOT a concept. A concept is a REPRESENTATION of the idea. Corollary: every idea need not be creative just because it is an "idea". A mere idea is not a design solution — an idea is the SEED of a creative solution.

## Class brainstorming exercise (group quiz, 1.5 h)

Each student writes one best benefit and one unsatisfactory point about the course (with WHY) → CR + 4 volunteers sort into positive vs unsatisfactory (binary) → sub-categories by content (e.g., "screen") → screenshots submitted. A quick brainstorm like this (who / what / when-where / why sticky notes) lets a team AGREE on a PROBLEM STATEMENT.

# 8.2 Creativity vs Innovation vs Invention

| Creativity | Innovation |
| Process of GENERATING ideas; rearranging thoughts to get new results | Tinkering, tweaking, refining, IMPLEMENTING those ideas; making something better out of everything |
| DIVERGENT thinking | CONVERGENT thinking |
| Effort towards better IDEAS | Effort towards putting ideas into ACTION |
| Often happens in a FLASH, as insight | A STEP-BY-STEP phenomenon |
| Ideas, imagination, thoughts, expression, ingenuity, brainstorming | Process, value, doing, implementation, useful, change, product, measurable |

- "With your THOUGHTS you are creative; with your ACTIONS you are innovative."
- Theodore Levitt: "Creativity is THINKING up new things. Innovation is DOING new things."
- They run in PARALLEL: Need → Ideation → Invention → Innovation → Society.

## Innovation vs Invention
- INNOVATION has a much broader meaning — it includes objects AND ideas, strategies, processes, conventions, techniques, methods, representations and ways of doing things.
- INVENTION = a NEW DEVICE or physical configuration purposefully built to improve a situation or solve a problem — a goal-directed PHYSICAL creation.

**Example:** The telephone was an invention; UPI payments (a new way of doing payments) is an innovation; the improved touch-screen smartphone is both.

## Design = Creation + Invention + Innovation

| Creation | Invention | Innovation |
| Non-existing | New | Novel (novelty = degree of newness) |
| Creative — thought process | Inventive — implementation | Innovative — improvement |

- Design — MANIFESTATION of all three.
- "Scientists DISCOVER, engineers INVENT, designers DESIGN, everyone INNOVATES!"
- Edison: invention is 1% INSPIRATION and 99% PERSPIRATION. Invention needs creative thinking + domain knowledge to solve problems: a MENTAL activity (creative thinking) + a PHYSICAL activity (innovative doing). Though inspiration is only 1%, its weightage is 99% in deciding QUALITY and success.
- "Quality of thinking increases the probability of innovating." "THINK with your hands — DO with your minds!"

# 8.4 Renovation, Kaizen, Jugaad and reverse engineering

## Renovation is an innovation (INCREMENTAL)
- When an existing product is improved in small phases while RETAINING its core, the innovation faces the LEAST RESISTANCE to change in an organisation. Example: your improved travel suitcase (wheels, then spinner wheels, then TSA lock).
- Innovation types: incremental, disruptive, radical, adjacent (judged by scope — product/business model; objective; boundaries — internal resources/external partners; intensity — how much change).

## KAIZEN (Japanese)
- KAI = change, ZEN = good → "good change" / continuous improvement; associated with LEAN methodology; origin in post-WWII Japanese quality circles.
- Big results come from MANY SMALL CHANGES accumulated over time through a PLANNED strategy.

## JUGAAD (Indian)
- India uses "abhikalpa" for design — but what for innovation? JUGAAD (Hindi) = a flexible approach to problem solving that uses LIMITED RESOURCES in an innovative way.
- Jugaad is DIFFERENT from kaizen. It is a one-off, by-chance, just-in-time attempt by enthusiastic hobbyists as a temporary alternative.
- Jugaad may be a STARTING point of innovation but NOT the ENDING point — so it is not really innovation; an innovation should have a planned end offering.
- It may not stand scientific scrutiny. "It works" does not mean it is a successful innovation. If adopted as a technical starting point it can be harmful and can short-circuit creative thinking by misleading perceptions.
- Example: a re-chargeable motorised tricycle built from scrap. IMPROVISATION is NOT innovation — at best the beginning of innovation.

## REVERSE ENGINEERING is not innovation
- Often mistaken for innovation. It is a LEARNING method to understand how a product works/is built. Just replacing parts with local materials is COPYING, not innovating.

## How to become innovative
- All inventions start from a CREATIVE solution.
- All inventions are innovations, but NOT all innovations are inventions.
- All innovations begin as creative solutions, but NOT all creative solutions become innovations.
- If one wants to DESIGN one has to INVENT; to invent one has to be INNOVATIVE; to be innovative one has to be CREATIVE.

# 9.5 Patents in detail
- Patents Act: a patent is a CONTRACT between an INVENTOR and the GOVERNMENT — the government grants EXCLUSIVE RIGHTS in exchange for a DETAILED DISCLOSURE of the invention.
- "Invention" means any new and useful (a) art, process, method or manner of manufacture, (b) machine, apparatus or other article/part, (c) substance produced by manufacture.

## Three conditions

1. NOVELTY — not part of the global STATE OF THE ART.

2. INVENTIVENESS (non-obviousness) — not obvious to a skilled person/expert in that area.

3. USEFULNESS — must have UTILITY value.
- Patent TERM: 20 YEARS. Once granted you can LICENSE it to yourself or others for commercialisation.

## Patent grant procedure (in brief)

Filing of patent application → Examination & novelty search → Acceptance or refusal → Notification of acceptance in the GAZETTE OF INDIA → Opposition (if any) → GRANT of patent.

## What CANNOT be patented (India)
- Substances intended as food, medicine or drug, or produced by chemical processes (as listed on the slide, incl. alloys, optical glass, semiconductors, inter-metallic compounds).
- Mere DISCOVERY of a scientific principle or abstract theory (e.g., the Raman effect).
- Mere discovery of a new property/new use of a known substance, or mere use of a known process/machine — unless it gives a new product or uses a new reactant.
- A substance from a mere ADMIXTURE (only aggregating properties of components).
- Mere ARRANGEMENT / rearrangement / duplication of known devices each working independently in a known way.
- A method of TESTING during manufacture to make a machine more efficient.
- A method of AGRICULTURE or HORTICULTURE.
- Any process for medical, surgical, curative, prophylactic or other TREATMENT of humans, animals or plants.
- Inventions relating to ATOMIC ENERGY.

# 9.6 Copyright, design patents and trademarks

## COPYRIGHT ACT (important for designers)
- Copyright = the economic rights given to creators of LITERARY and ARTISTIC works — to reproduce, copy, perform or display the work publicly.
- It protects the "EXPRESSION", not the content. Example: a photograph's expression is protected, not the Brahmaputra or the water bottle in it.
- It does NOT protect new ideas and processes. It acknowledges AUTHORSHIP; it doesn't stop copying, but if someone passes your work off as theirs you can file a case.
- Covered works: musical, dramatic, artistic works; photography, painting, DESIGN SKETCHES, architectural drawings, mechanical sketches, poems, SOFTWARE program manuscripts, scripts, digital medium/UI, animations, typography, colour combinations.
- TERM: life of the author + 60 years.
- Registration is NOT required — copyright arises AUTOMATICALLY when the work is created — but registering is advisable (otherwise you have no proof of being the creator).
- Papers, theses, public presentations, speeches, essays, curricula, photographs, lecture content are all under copyright — that is why names and dates are put on PPTs.

## DESIGN PATENTS (Design registration in India)
- Protect only FORM or APPEARANCE (aesthetics); they CANNOT protect function or structure (that is the job of utility patents in the US / patents in India).
- Example: the iPhone's continuous glass surface and distinctive shape are protected by several design patents; its "swipe to unlock" feature is protected by a UTILITY patent.
- The DRAWINGS define the legal boundaries of a design patent → a designer must SKETCH and PRESERVE drawings WITH DATES. Examples: Coca-Cola bottle, car body, chair.

## TRADEMARKS
- Commercial SOURCE INDICATORS — distinctive signs identifying goods/services of a specific person or enterprise.
- A word, phrase, symbol or design, or a combination. Includes well-known marks, domain names, geographical indications, non-traditional marks.
- Quick quiz: Are LOGOS protectable under trademarks? — YES (e.g., the IIITDMJ logo).

# 9.8 Creative Commons and AI

## Creative Commons (CC)
- An American non-profit (founded 2001; Lawrence Lessig) that releases free COPYRIGHT LICENCES so authors can state which rights they RESERVE and which they WAIVE — "some rights reserved" instead of "all rights reserved". Part of the COPYLEFT movement; ~2 billion works use CC licences (Flickr, YouTube, Wikipedia, Vimeo, MIT OpenCourseWare, Sketchfab).
- Why creators use CC: share widely while keeping credit; encourage remixing and learning; reach global audiences; support open education and culture; build visibility and reputation.

| Licence (most → least permissive) | Allows |
| CC BY | Share, remix, build upon — even commercially — with CREDIT |
| CC BY-SA | As above, but adaptations must be SHARED ALIKE (same licence) |
| CC BY-ND | Copy & distribute (commercial OK) with credit, NO DERIVATIVES |
| CC BY-NC | Remix with credit, NON-COMMERCIAL only |
| CC BY-NC-SA | Non-commercial, share-alike |
| CC BY-NC-ND | Most restrictive: copy with credit only, non-commercial, no adaptations |

- BY = attribution; SA = share-alike; NC = non-commercial; ND = no derivatives.

## AI and IPR
- Under existing copyright laws, ONLY HUMANS are treated as creators. A copyright office rejected an AI system (DABUS) as the author of an artwork.
- AI tools used in IPR work: The Lens (patent search & analysis), PatentPal (drafting patent claims), Clarivate (patent intelligence), TrademarkNow (trademark search & alerts), Markify (global trademark monitoring), Turnitin (plagiarism detection), Copyleaks (content originality).
