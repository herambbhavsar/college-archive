# UNIT A — FOUNDATIONS: EYES, SENSES AND INFORMATION

# T1 Visual Ergonomics and Introduction

Source: Jeffrey Anshel, Visual Ergonomics Handbook, Chapter 1 "Windows to the World".

## What is Visual Ergonomics?

Ergonomics means fitting the task, product or environment to the human, not forcing the human to fit the task. Visual ergonomics is the part of ergonomics that deals with how we SEE: making sure that what people have to look at (signs, screens, labels, maps, displays, rooms) matches how the human eye and brain actually work.
- The eyes are "simple tools designed to catch light". What makes vision a wonder is how the eye gathers, filters and guides light, and how the brain processes it.
- Of all the senses, the eyes contribute the most when we interact with our environment. How well we use our eyes decides how well we survive and perform.
- To understand how the eyes are designed to work, Anshel looks at our history — physical development and social development.

**Example:** A railway platform display, a medicine label and a phone screen are all visual-ergonomics problems — each must be readable quickly, without strain, by many kinds of people.

## 1. Our Ancestral Eyes (the "hunter-gatherer" eye)
- Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared about 40,000 years ago. Priorities were food, shelter, protection and survival; comfort came later. The body developed flexibility, agility and strength; people lived only about 25–30 years.
- The eyes were designed for the same survival needs:
- Frontal position: both eyes face forward, so their visual fields overlap → stereoscopic vision = ability to perceive depth.
- Placed near the top of the body → longest range of sight.
- Daytime vision was sharp (keen); night vision was only adequate (secondary).
- Motility: eyes move in many directions, together with the head, to scan a wide horizon.
- KEY POINT: we still use this same "hunter-gatherer" visual system today.

## 2. The mismatch today
- Today we look at our world about 16 hours a day, much of it under artificial light, mostly at objects within arm's reach.
- We read small text in different lighting for hours and work to deadlines. For much of the year our eyes get very little daylight.
- The eyes must adapt to these adverse conditions. Adaptation is slow and not always successful; often we sacrifice one kind of vision (e.g., distance) to see better in another (near work).

**Example:** A student who studies on a laptop 10 hours a day uses eyes evolved for scanning a distant horizon in daylight — this mismatch causes eyestrain, dryness and headaches.

## 3. The Information Age — three big shifts in how we use our eyes

| Period | What changed | Visual demand |
| Agricultural society (till late 1800s) | Hunting + farming | Clear distance vision in daylight, occasional near viewing |
| Late 1870s – Industrial Age | Light bulb extended viewing into night; machines, automobile, airplane | Driving = distance + intermediate (distance most critical); close inspection of machine parts, dials → near-point viewing became critical |
| Mid-20th century – Information Age | Computer | Self-illuminated VDT (video display terminal) screen, awkward viewing angle, unusual working distance |

- In the industrial age, a worker's success depended on how efficiently he used his eyes. Poor vision → lower performance and productivity + higher risk of accidents.
- The computer shift came less than 100 years after the industrial shift — extremely fast in historical terms, so the eye has had very little time to adapt.

## 4. An Eye on the Future (why visual ergonomics matters now)
- Computer users: about 10% of the workforce in 1980 → more than 80% at the time of writing.
- 1991 Lou Harris poll: computer-related eyestrain was the No. 1 office health complaint in the USA.
- 1991 NIOSH study: 88% of the 66 million people who used computers more than 3 hours a day (nearly 60 million people) had eyestrain symptoms.
- 2004 survey (1000+ consumers): more than 61% worried about vision problems from long computer use; more than 60% believed it would get worse.
- Eyestrain overtook carpal tunnel syndrome as a complaint but gets less attention because eye problems are usually painless and slow to develop, so they are misdiagnosed as migraine or tiredness.
- Children: growing up full-time on computers and games; furniture, monitor placement, mouse size, lighting and posture for children are not yet considered.
- Eye-care practitioners must analyse how patients interact with their environment, help design optimal viewing environments and evaluate them.
- Future: just as sunglasses are normal for sunny days, computer-viewing glasses may become normal. Whatever the input device (even voice), the OUTPUT of computers will always use the visual system.

# T2 Special about the Senses

Source: Anshel Ch. 1 (same chapter) + supporting points from Human Factors in Engineering & Design (Sanders & McCormick) Ch. 3.

## Why vision is "special"
- Of all senses, vision contributes the most to how we deal with our environment; it can take in a large amount of information at the same time (simultaneously).
- The eye is only a light-catching tool; "seeing" really happens in the brain, which processes what the eye collects.
- Special features of human vision (from Topic 1): stereoscopic depth perception from two overlapping eyes; long-range view from the high position; sharp daytime vision; eye movement + head movement to scan widely.
- The eye catches light → light is turned into nerve signals → the brain builds our picture of the world.

## The senses as information channels
- We are constantly bombarded by stimuli from the environment and from inside our body. Sense organs receive them: eyes, ears, skin receptors, and proprioceptors in muscles (body position sense). The brain processes them.
- The most powerful information processor on earth is still the human brain.
- Each sense has strengths: vision for complex, spatial, long information; hearing for short, urgent, attention-grabbing signals; touch for special cases (blind users, or when eyes and ears are overloaded). (Details in Topic 4.)

**Example:** In a car the eyes read the road and speedometer, the ears pick up a horn or an indicator click, and the hands feel the steering vibration — three senses working as parallel information channels.

# T3 Visual Ergonomics: Pertinence of the Eyes

Source: Human Factors in Engineering and Design (HFED), Part II Information Input, pp. 47, 51–53, 58–59 (+ eye structure pp. 92–93).

## 1. Information input and processing (p. 47)
- We live in an "information revolution", but the best information processor is still the human brain.
- Stimuli reach us through sense organs (eyes, ears, skin, muscle proprioceptors) and are processed by the brain.
- The study of human information processing is called cognitive psychology / cognitive engineering / engineering psychology. Human factors helped this field grow by focusing on practical problems of perception and attention.

## 2. How the eye works (pp. 92–93) — why the eye matters for design
- Cornea → aqueous humor → pupil (size set by the iris: about 8 mm in the dark, about 2 mm in bright light) → lens → vitreous humor → retina.
- The lens changes shape: muscles contract → lens bulges for near objects; relax → lens flattens for far objects.
- Retina has two kinds of receptors:

| Cones | Rods |
| About 6–7 million, packed in the centre (fovea) | About 130 million, mostly in the periphery (max at 10–20° from fovea) |
| Work in bright light (day) | Work in dim light (night) |
| See colour and fine detail | See only shades of black and white |

- The fovea is the area of sharpest vision: to see something clearly it needs enough light for the cones and must be focused on the fovea.
- Because rods are in the periphery, a dim object is seen better by looking slightly to one side of it (astronomers do this with dim stars).
- Eye vs camera: eye focuses faster (lens doesn't move back and forth); works over a much wider range of light (rods + cones); two eyes see 3D; and the "film" is put in backwards — receptors face the back of the eye, under blood vessels and nerves.
- Accommodation = the lens's ability to focus. Near point = closest distance you can focus; far point = farthest (near infinity in normal eyes). Dark focus = where the eye relaxes in darkness, about arm's length (1 m). Focus is measured in diopters: 1 D = 1 m, 2 D = 0.5 m, 3 D = 0.33 m.

**Example:** Older people hold a newspaper at arm's length because their near point has moved away — so medicine labels for the elderly need bigger text.

## 3. Displaying information (p. 51)
- Information can come to us directly (watching a plane) or indirectly through a device (radar, telescope). Indirect stimuli are either:
- Coded stimuli — visual or auditory displays (a warning light, a siren).
- Reproduced stimuli — TV, radio, photographs, microscope, hearing aid (may be enlarged, filtered or enhanced).
- Design matters in indirect sensing, because this is where designers create displays.
- Display = any indirect way of presenting information: a road sign, a radio, a page of braille.

## 4. Dynamic vs static information
- Dynamic information changes over time: traffic lights, speedometer, radar, temperature gauge.
- Static information stays fixed (for a time): printed text, traffic signs, charts, graphs, labels.
- On computer screens (VDTs) the line is blurred, but text on a screen is still treated as static because it is replaced, not continuously changed.

## 5. Eight types of displayed information (pp. 52–53)

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| Quantitative | Exact value of a variable | Thermometer reading 37.2°C, speed 60 km/h |
| Qualitative | Approximate value, trend, rate or direction of change | Is engine temperature rising? |
| Status | Condition of a system, one of a few states | ON/OFF, stop–caution–go lights, TV channel |
| Warning and signal | Emergency/unsafe conditions or presence of something | Fire alarm light, lighthouse beacon |
| Representational | Pictures or graphics of objects/areas | Maps, photos, blueprints, heartbeat on a monitor, radar blips |
| Identification | Identifies an object or condition, usually coded | Colour-coded pipes, lane markings, hazard labels |
| Alphanumeric and symbolic | Words, numbers, codes | Signs, labels, instructions, braille, music notes, moving news text |
| Time-phased | Pulsed signals with set durations and gaps | Morse code, blinking lights |

- One display can show more than one type, and users use information for more than one purpose. The type of information often decides the type of display.

## 6. Choosing the sense (modality) — Table 3-1 (p. 53)

| Use AUDITORY (sound) when… | Use VISUAL when… |
| Message is simple | Message is complex |
| Message is short | Message is long |
| Won't be referred to later | Will be referred to later |
| Deals with events in time | Deals with location in space |
| Calls for immediate action | Does not need immediate action |
| Visual system is overburdened | Auditory system is overburdened |
| Place is too bright, or dark adaptation must be kept | Place is too noisy |
| Person keeps moving around | Person stays in one position |

- Hearing is better for vigilance tasks because it grabs attention.
- Touch (tactual) is rarely used, except for blind people or when eyes and ears are overloaded.

**Example:** Train announcement "Platform changed — train arriving now" = auditory (short, urgent). The full timetable = visual (long, complex, referred to later).

## 7. Compatibility (pp. 58–59)

Compatibility = how well stimuli and responses match human expectations. The more compatible, the less mental "recoding" → faster learning, faster response, fewer errors, lower mental workload, and people simply like it more. (Term by A. M. Small; refined by Fitts & Seeger, 1953.)

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| Conceptual | Codes/symbols match what people already associate with them | An aeroplane symbol for an airport on a map (not a green square); meaningful computer command abbreviations |
| Movement | Movement of controls/displays matches system response | Turning a knob clockwise increases volume; pointer moving up = value increasing |
| Spatial | Physical arrangement of controls matches their displays | Five knobs in a row directly below five displays (not in a column at the side) |
| Modality | Some stimulus–response sense combinations suit some tasks better | Verbal task → spoken input + spoken response; spatial task → visual input + manual response (Wickens et al., 1983) |

# T4 Nonvisual Modalities

Source: same HFED pages as Topic 3 (pp. 47, 51–53, 58–59). Focus here is on the senses OTHER than vision.

## Key points
- Sense organs other than eyes: ears (auditory), skin receptors (touch/tactual, temperature), proprioceptors (body position), and also smell (olfactory). HFED covers auditory, tactual and olfactory displays in its Chapter 6 and speech displays in Chapter 7.
- When to use sound instead of vision → the 8 rules of Table 3-1 (see Topic 3). In short: SHORT, SIMPLE, URGENT, TIME-BASED messages, or when the eyes are busy, the place is too bright/dark, or the person moves around.
- Hearing has an attention-getting quality → better for vigilance tasks (long watch tasks where a rare signal must not be missed).
- Touch (tactual displays): used for blind persons (braille) and in special cases where vision and hearing are overloaded (e.g., shaped control knobs a pilot can identify without looking).
- Modality compatibility: match the sense of input to the response. Verbal tasks work best with speech in and speech out; spatial tasks work best with visual display and manual response.
- Time-phased signals (Morse code, pulsed sounds) and warning signals are often non-visual.
- From Ergonomics for Beginners (p. 52): sound should be reserved for warning signals — the ear catches sound from all directions so a person cannot "escape" it and need not be at a particular spot; but too many sounds irritate over time.

**Examples:** phone vibrating in your pocket (touch), reversing beeper on a truck (sound), smell added to cooking gas so a leak is noticed (olfactory warning), raised dots on the F and J keys (touch).

# UNIT B — TEXT, TYPE, SYMBOLS, COLOUR AND LAYOUT

# T5 Icons, Symbols and Comprehension

Source: HFED pp. 92–93, 99, 103–106, 108–110, 128 (+ the symbols section of the same chapter, pp. 120–127, which explains this topic directly).

## 1. Basic visual capabilities behind comprehension (pp. 92–93, 99)
- Accommodation (focusing), near point, far point, dark focus (see Topic 3).
- Visual acuity improves with exposure time up to about 100–200 ms, then levels off.
- Target motion reduces acuity. Dynamic visual acuity = seeing detail while you or the target moves (reading roadside signs from a car). Acuity falls fast above about 60°/s.
- Age: acuity and contrast sensitivity decline after 40; by 75, acuity is about 20/30. Displays for elderly users need large targets and good lighting — and these also help young users.
- Training can improve focus control and acuity (Roscoe & Couchman: Snellen acuity +14%, contrast sensitivity +32%).
- Adaptation: entering a dark cinema — cones adapt in about 5 min, rods in about 30–35 min.

## 2. Symbols are a form of coding

Good coding (and so good symbols) follows 5 guidelines: detectability, discriminability, compatibility, meaningfulness, standardization.

## 3. How symbols are tested (criteria)
- Recognition — show the symbol, ask what it means.
- Matching — match symbols to a list of meanings (time and errors may be measured).
- Preferences and opinions — ask which design people prefer.

## 4. Two classic studies
- Mandatory-action symbols (Cairney & Siess, 1982): safety symbols (wear ear, eye, foot protection etc.) shown to newly arrived Vietnamese people in Australia. Most were understood and, more importantly, remembered a week later → a good symbol may not be obvious at first but must be EASY TO LEARN.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_000.jpg|Mandatory-action symbols with % recognised (O) and recalled a week later (R) (HFED Fig. 4-16)]
- Exit symbols (Collins & Lerner, 1983): 18 exit-sign designs tested under difficult viewing. Findings: filled figures beat outline figures; square/rectangular backgrounds beat circles; simpler figures with fewer elements work better. "No exit" signs were sometimes confused with "Exit".
[FIG:fig/d/ve_001.jpg|Exit-sign designs with % error — filled figures and square backgrounds did better (HFED Fig. 4-17)]

## 5. Perceptual principles of symbol design (Easterby)

| Principle | Meaning |
| Figure–ground | Symbol must stand clearly apart from its background; an unstable figure makes an arrow's direction ambiguous |
| Figure boundary | A solid (contrast) shape is better than a line outline |
| Closure | Closed shapes are perceived more easily than open/broken ones |
| Simplicity | As simple as possible, keeping only necessary features |
| Unity | Symbol parts should form one unit (solid figure placed inside the outline figure) |

- Standardization: one symbol must always mean the same thing — e.g., international road signs, US National Park Service symbols for picnic area, cycle trail, playground.

**Examples:** the toilet man/woman pictogram, the Wi-Fi symbol, the "no smoking" circle-slash, the green running-man exit sign.

## 6. Codes
- Referent = the thing being coded (a pipe, a type of road sign). Coding dimension = the visual feature used: colour, shape, letters, numbers, flash rate, angle, size.
- Example: an octagon road sign = STOP.
- Smith & Thomas (1964): in a counting/search task, COLOUR codes were best at all display densities.
- How many levels people can tell apart (Table 4-5):

| Code | Levels possible | Preferable limit / comment |
| Alphanumeric | 10 numerals, 26 letters, unlimited combos | Good for identification, little space; some characters confused |
| Colour of surfaces | 9 hues; 24+ with hue/saturation/brightness | Limit 9; best for search & counting; problem for colour-blind |
| Colour of lights | 10 | Limit 3 |
| Geometric shapes | 15+ | Limit 5; good in symbolic displays |
| Angle of inclination | 24 | Limit 12; e.g., clock hands, direction |
| Size of forms | 5–6 | Limit 3; takes space |
| Visual number | 6 | Limit 4 |
| Brightness of lights | 3–4 | Limit 2 |
| Flash rate | — | Limit 2; useful to attract attention |

- Colour is best for searching tasks (Christ, 1975, 42 studies), but for identifying meaning, letters and numbers are better (e.g., "Grade A", "size 12").
- Multidimension codes: do not combine more than two dimensions if quick reading is needed; some combinations don't work together.

## 7. Page 128 — the big picture

We are bombarded with text, graphics, symbols and codes. Software and manuals succeed or fail on how clearly information is presented, so screen layouts and instruction manuals are key areas for human factors.

# T6 Graphic Design and Typeface: the Visual Ergonomic Angle

Source: same HFED pages as Topic 5 (92–93, 99, 103–106, 108–110, 128). Here the focus is on TYPE.

## 1. Three criteria for any text: visibility, legibility, readability
- Visibility — can it be seen at all? Legibility — can each character be told apart? Readability — can words and sentences be understood easily?
- Typography choices matter most in 4 conditions: (1) poor viewing (bad light, little time), (2) important/critical information (emergency labels), (3) viewing at a distance, (4) users with poor vision. Otherwise typeface variety is mostly an aesthetic choice.

## 2. Stroke width (Fig. 4-6, 4-7)
- Stroke width = thickness of the letter's stroke ÷ letter height (e.g., 1:6).
- Irradiation: white letters on black appear to "spread" into the dark area; black on white doesn't. So black-on-white letters should be thicker than white-on-black ones.

| Condition | Recommended stroke width : height |
| Black on white, good light | 1:6 to 1:8 |
| White on black, good light | 1:8 to 1:10 |
| Low light or low contrast | Bold, about 1:5 |
| Highly luminous letters | 1:12 to 1:20 |
| Black letters on a very bright background | Very thick strokes |

- As light reduces, thick letters become more readable than thin ones.

## 3. Width-to-height ratio
- A 3:5 ratio is common and supported by research (letter B has 5 elements in height, 3 in width).
- Heglin: better to fit letter width to its geometry — O a perfect circle, A and V like equilateral triangles.
- Wider letters (1:1) suit transilluminated (back-lit) characters and engraved labels (military MIL-M-18012B / NAMEL font).
[FIG:fig/d/ve_002.jpg|Stroke width-to-height ratios: black on white vs white on black (HFED Fig. 4-7)]

## 4. Styles of type (over 30,000 typefaces), four classes

| Class | Features | Use |
| Roman | Has serifs (small flourishes) | Most common, conventional body text |
| Gothic (sans serif) | Uniform stroke, no serifs | Labels, signs, headings |
| Script | Imitates handwriting | Wedding invitations |
| Block letter | Like 15th-century German manuscript | Decorative |

- Italics: emphasis, titles, special words. Boldface: headings, labels, emphasis, and legibility in poor conditions.

## 5. Type size (pp. 105–106)
- Point size: 1 pt = 1/72 inch (0.35 mm), but this is the height of the slug (includes ascenders and descenders). For actual capital letter height use 1 pt ≈ 1/100 inch (0.25 mm).
- Normal reading distance 12–16 inches (nominal 14 in / 35.5 cm). Newspaper/magazine 9–11 pt is suitable for general printed text. Use larger type when reading is critical, lighting is poor or characters change (instruments).
- For distance signs, design for a Snellen acuity of 20/40 (Table 4-3: e.g., at 20 ft letters about 0.8–1.4 in high depending on stroke ratio; at 100 ft about 4–7 in).
- Larger letters are needed for low contrast or poor illumination.

# T7 Visual Ergonomics of Typography and Colour

Source: HFED pp. 103–106, 108–110 (+ colour-code points from pp. 125–127).

## 1. Typography points (pp. 103–106): stroke width, width-height ratio, type styles, size — see Topic 6.

## 2. Case (p. 108)
- lowercase is easier to read than ALL CAPITALS (Poulton, 1967), because the word shape ("envelope") is more distinctive: "pie" vs "tie" look different because of p's descender and t's ascender; "PIE" and "TIE" look alike.
- Labels on control panels are often in capitals to maximise letter height in a small space. For SEARCH tasks (finding a label), the first letter matters most — a larger initial letter helps (Phillips, 1979).

## 3. Layout (pp. 108–109)
- Interletter spacing: close-set (high-density) type was read faster than regular spacing in a brochure study (Moriarty & Scheiner, 1984) — more letters fall on the fovea per glance, fewer eye jumps (saccades).
- Interline spacing: lines of text have a striped pattern that can cause visual discomfort, eyestrain and headaches (Wilkins & Nimmo-Smith, 1987); more space between lines improves clarity.

## 4. Reading ease (pp. 109–110)
- Readability also depends on writing style, sentence construction and content.
- Simple, affirmative, active sentences are easiest. Active: "The green button starts the motor." Passive: "The motor is started by the green button." Negative: "The red button does not start the motor."
- Order of words must match order of actions. Bad: "Push the green button after turning off the gas." Good: "Turn off the gas before pushing the green button."
- Flesch Reading Ease Score (100-word sample): Score = 206.835 − (0.846 × syllables) − (1.015 × average words per sentence).

| Score | Difficulty | Typical material |
| 0–30 | Very difficult | Scientific |
| 30–50 | Difficult | Academic |
| 50–60 | Fairly difficult | Quality |
| 60–70 | Standard | Digests |
| 70–80 | Fairly easy | Slick fiction |
| 80–90 | Easy | Pulp fiction |
| 90–100 | Very easy | Comics |

- Limitation: shuffling words randomly doesn't change the score but destroys understanding.

## 5. Colour (from the same chapter)
- Colour coding is best for search and counting tasks; people can identify about 9 surface colours absolutely (more with training); for untrained users use only a few distinct colours.
- Colour has problems with some lighting and with colour-defective (colour-blind) people → always use colour redundantly with text or shape.
- For identification of meaning, letters and numerals beat colour.
- Contrast: luminance contrast between text and background is essential (contrast even matters more than lighting — Ergonomics for Beginners).

**Examples:** Metro lines coded by colour AND name; Indian traffic signal (colour + position: red top, green bottom); a "SALE" poster in red with white bold text.

# T8 Visual Ergonomics of Alignment

Source: Jan Dul & Bernard Weerdmeester, Ergonomics for Beginners, pp. 46–49, 52.

## 1. Visual information (p. 46)
- The eyes are the most important information source: they take in a LARGE amount of information at the same time. People with limited eyesight miss information or take longer → information must suit as many people as possible.
- The guidelines apply to screens, books, newspapers, magazines, overhead sheets, presentations, slides and posters.

## 2. Guidelines for characters and text layout
[FIG:fig/d/ve_003.jpg|ALL CAPITALS (a) vs lowercase (b) — lowercase gives each word a shape (Ergonomics for Beginners Fig. 3.2)]

| Guideline | Explanation | Example |
| Do not use text entirely in CAPITALS | Lowercase letters with ascenders (b d f h k l t) and descenders (g j p q y) give each word a recognisable shape; reader sees words at a glance. Use capitals only for first letters, titles, proper nouns, familiar abbreviations | "Platform 4" not "PLATFORM 4" in long text |
| Do not justify text by inserting blank spaces | Right-justifying by stretching spaces makes uneven gaps and hurts legibility → use LEFT-ALIGNED, ragged-right text. Proportional spacing (less space for i, more for m, w) is pleasant but not essential | Newspaper columns with "rivers" of white space are harder to read |
| Use a familiar, plain typeface | Characters without much ornamentation are most legible; sans-serif preferred for headings, captions, directions, names, titles | Signboards in Helvetica/Arial-type fonts |
| Avoid confusion between characters | Fewer points forming a character → more confusion. Risky pairs in codes/abbreviations: 0/O, 1/I/l, 5/S, 8/B, 2/Z | Ticket PNR codes avoid letters like O and I |
| Size characters properly | Capital letter height ≥ about 1/200 of reading distance (20 m room → letters ≥ 10 cm on screen). On screens, capitals ≥ 3 mm. Base capital proportions on "O", lowercase on "o", stroke width on "I" | Classroom slides readable from the last bench |
| The longer the line, the more line spacing | Line spacing should be at least about 1/30 of line length; newspaper columns (short lines) can use less spacing than a book. Too close → eye loses its place when returning to the next line | Wide website text needs more line spacing |
| Good contrast | Difference in brightness between text and background; contrast affects legibility even more than lighting. Fig. 3.7: dark-on-light and light-on-dark with strong contrast (examples 1 and 10) are most legible | Grey text on a light grey background is hard to read |

## 3. Hearing (p. 52)
- The ear is not used much for information except speech; if the eyes are overtaxed, sound can help.
- Avoid frequent sound signals — even pleasant sounds irritate over time.
- Computer speech can now sound human; sometimes the user must know it is a machine because it changes how they react (telephone voice-response systems).
- Sound should be RESERVED FOR WARNING SIGNALS: sounds reach us from all directions, we cannot escape them and need not be at a particular spot. In monitoring tasks, missed signals increase over time if only vision is used; a bell or buzzer at the limit makes it easier.
- Fig. 3.9: adapt the display to its objective (choose digital, analog or warning display based on what the user needs).

# UNIT C — DISPLAYS AND CRITICAL INFORMATION

# T10 Digital Displays and Railway Reservation Form

Source: HFED pp. 133–135, 146–148, 152, 176, 178 (Table 6-2 only).

## 1. Know the user's information needs first (p. 133)
- Situation awareness = perceiving elements, understanding their meaning and predicting their near-future state (air traffic controller predicting where planes will be in 5–15 min).
- Failure to understand all information needs → incomplete display.
- Car engine temperature: a warning light ("idiot light") gives check reading (overheated or not), but drivers also need qualitative reading (is it rising?) → so a temperature gauge is still needed.
- Second hand on a watch is mainly a check that the watch is running; that's why the colon blinks on digital watches.

## 2. Quantitative displays (pp. 133–135)

Three basic types:

1. Fixed scale with moving pointer (analog)

2. Moving scale with fixed pointer (analog)

3. Digital display (counter)
- DIGITAL is better when (1) a precise number is needed and (2) the value stays long enough to be read. Simmonds et al. (1981): digital speedometer better for accuracy and preference.
- ANALOG (fixed scale, moving pointer) is better when values change often/continuously and when direction or rate of change matters (qualitative reading).
- Fixed scale + moving pointer is generally better than moving scale + fixed pointer.

Heglin's 5 rules for analog displays:

1. A pointer moving against a fixed scale is preferred.

2. If increase relates to "more/up", use a straight vertical (thermometer) scale with moving pointer.

3. Don't mix types of moving-element indicators for related functions (avoids reversal errors).

4. If the user controls it manually, let the control move the pointer, not the scale.

5. Small, variable changes are more visible with a moving pointer.

## 3. Qualitative reading with coded zones (pp. 146–147)
- Colour-coded zones on a scale (e.g., green normal, yellow caution, red danger) let the user understand the meaning instantly without first reading a number and then deciding its range.
- Coded zones can still show trends and, with scale values, exact readings.
- Check reading = is the reading normal? Normal value must be clearly coded.
- Panels of dials: align all "normal" positions at 9 o'clock (or 12 o'clock) → gestalt: the whole pattern is seen as one, so a deviating dial stands out at a glance. Extended lines between dials make it even clearer.

**Example:** A car dashboard where all warning needles point the same way when normal; power-plant control panels.

## 4. Status indicators (p. 148)
- Show separate, discrete states: on/off, stop/caution/go.
- If an instrument is used only for check reading, convert it to a status indicator (simpler).
- Traffic lights use REDUNDANT coding: colour (red, yellow, green) + position (top, middle, bottom) — works for colour-blind drivers too.

## 5. Signal and warning lights (pp. 148, 152)
- Detectability depends on size, luminance and exposure time together: bigger or longer-lasting lights need less brightness; for real use, luminance should be at least double the 50%-detection threshold to be seen 99% of the time.
- Guidelines: if flash rates represent levels, use no more than 3 rates; warning light at least twice as bright as the background; within 30° of the normal line of sight; normally RED (other signal lights in the area should be other colours); at least 1° of visual angle in size.

## 6. Representational displays (p. 152)
- Moving elements on a background (aircraft display showing other aircraft over terrain). Purpose: situation awareness.
- Zones of interest (Fracker): your car → vehicles around you → road system/city → your goals and route.
- Endsley: we match display information to learned "templates" (schema) in memory to understand and predict — a line of red tail-lights means traffic will slow and cars will change lanes.

## 7. Auditory display principles (p. 176)

General: Compatibility (high pitch = up/high; wailing = emergency); Approximation (two-stage signals: attention signal + designation signal); Dissociability (easily told apart from other sounds); Parsimony (no more info than necessary); Invariance (same signal = same meaning always).

Presentation: avoid extremes (very loud startles); set intensity relative to ambient noise; use interrupted/variable rather than steady signals (prevents adaptation); don't overload the auditory channel (Three Mile Island nuclear accident had 60+ alarms at once).

Installation: test the signals with representative users.

## 8. Table 6-2: types of audio alarms (p. 178)

| Alarm | Intensity | Frequency | Attention-getting | Noise-penetration |
| Diaphone (foghorn) | Very high | Very low | Good | Poor in low-frequency noise |
| Horn | High | Low to high | Good | Good |
| Whistle | High | Low to high | Good if intermittent | Good if frequency chosen properly |
| Siren | High | Low to high | Very good if pitch rises and falls | Very good with rising/falling frequency |
| Bell | Medium | Medium to high | Good | Good in low-frequency noise |
| Buzzer | Low to medium | Low to medium | Good | Fair if spectrum suits background noise |
| Chimes and gong | Low to medium | Low to medium | Fair | Fair if spectrum suits background noise |
| Oscillator | Low to high | Medium to high | Good if intermittent | Good if frequency chosen properly |

## 9. Applying this to a Railway Reservation Form (class discussion)

The same principles judge an IRCTC/railway reservation form:
- Group fields in the order the user thinks and acts (journey details → passenger details → contact → payment) — order of information = order of action (Topic 7).
- Clear labels in sentence case, left-aligned, adequate size and contrast (Topic 8); avoid confusable characters in codes (PNR, train numbers: 0/O, 1/I).
- Use digital display for exact values (fare, seat number, PNR); status indicators for availability (AVAILABLE / RAC / WL with colour + text, redundant coding); warning colour only for errors.
- Example of a good change: showing "WL 23" in red AND text, "Available 45" in green AND text.

# T12 Critical Procedures: "Better Drug Labeling for Pharmacists"

Source: Quinn, Bojko, Gaddy & Israelski, Ergonomics in Design, Winter 2006.

## 1. The problem
- US pharmacies: about 51.5 million dispensing errors in 3 billion prescriptions a year (Flynn et al., 2003) → 98.3% accuracy = 1.7% error rate.
- Looks small, but a pharmacy filling 250 prescriptions a day makes about 4 errors a day. Chance of a wrongly filled new prescription: 1 in 30; clinically important error: 1 in 1000 (about 3.3 million important errors).
- Many factors cause errors (work practices, facility design); this study focuses on one: the manufacturer's label on the drug bottle.

## 2. Who uses the label?
- Pharmacists and pharmacy technicians — to pick the correct drug from the shelf and tell it apart from look-alike labels. Bar codes help, but staff still rely on the physical label. They then add a patient-specific pharmacy label.
- A major manufacturer found its labels were inconsistent (because it had bought other brands over time); some had small text, some dark text on dark backgrounds.
- FDA gives templates for over-the-counter "Drug Facts", but labels for professionals are left to each manufacturer.

## 3. Nine types of dispensing errors (Flynn et al.)

(a) wrong drug, (b) wrong strength, (c) wrong dosage form (e.g., sustained release), (d) wrong quantity, (e) wrong prescription label info (labelled for husband instead of wife), (f) wrong label instructions, (g) omission, (h) wrong time, (i) deteriorated drug (expired/badly stored).

The study targeted the 4 a manufacturer label can influence: wrong drug, wrong strength, wrong dosage form, deteriorated drug.

## 4. Anatomy of a manufacturer's label

National Drug Code (NDC); brand name; generic/chemical name; dosage formulation (extended release); dosage strength (100 mg, 5 ml); quantity and form (100 tablets); controlled-substance symbol; "Rx only"; manufacturer name/location; expiration date; lot number; storage information. Some add a picture of the tablet or special notices (colour change, FDA warning).

## 5. 30 design guidelines (from human factors + pharmacy literature, incl. aviation labelling guidance)

| # | Guideline | Reason |
| 1 | Brand name = largest element | Attract attention, readability |
| 2 | Generic name equal or slightly smaller than brand | Pharmacists rely on it too |
| 3 | Extended-release shown near the name | Avoid confusing formulations |
| 4 | Highlight look-alike/sound-alike names | Avoid name confusion |
| 5 | Dosage strength near the name, as prominent or highlighted | Avoid strength confusion |
| 6 | Standard units of measure (US Pharmacopoeia) | Avoid strength confusion |
| 7 | NDC at the top | Cross-check with pharmacy label |
| 8 | Quantity and form below strength | Correct amount |
| 9 | Expiry date and lot number on front | Avoid deterioration; recalls |
| 10 | Storage on front if not room temperature | Avoid deterioration |
| 11 | Warnings prominent but not blocking name/strength | Safety |
| 12 | "Rx only" near bottom | Separate from over-the-counter |
| 13 | Scheduled-drug symbol near bottom, emphasised | Controlled substances |
| 14 | Manufacturer name/logo present | Confirmation, recalls |
| 15 | Read horizontally, not vertically | Readable on a shelf |
| 16 | About 8th-grade reading level | Professionals under stress |
| 17 | Dark letters on light background | Contrast in all lighting |
| 18 | Front-panel text at least 7 pt | Readability; space for translation |
| 19 | One font family (except brand) | Fewer distractions |
| 20 | Colour coding redundant with text | Colour-blind users, low light |
| 21 | Left-aligned on flat surfaces | Easier scanning, less clutter |
| 22 | Upper + lowercase, not ALL CAPS (but "WARNING" in caps) | Word-shape recognition |
| 23 | Avoid italics | Harder to read |

## 6. The new template
- Lower information density; black text on white; large, readable font for the most important items in order: brand name → generic name → dosage strength → NDC; colour coding with enough contrast for dosage strengths; RED for dosage form and alerts; optional tablet picture.
- They tested one exception to the guidelines: light text on dark colour-coded backgrounds for strength (common practice, never tested).
- Minimum version (basic elements) and maximum version (all elements). Labels shown at 2 × 3 inches. Imaginary drug names (anagrams of real ones, same letters and length) to remove familiarity effects.

## 7. The test
- 20 pharmacists and technicians, 1-hour session, two parts: (1) real bottles — participants touched the label as if picking from a shelf; time measured by a researcher who could not see the bottles (to avoid bias); (2) on-screen eye tracking — number of fixations (how much scanning) and fixation duration (longer = unclear or dense information).
- 60 labels in sets of three, 34 tasks (find brand name, generic name, ER/CR formulation, dosage strength, NDC, expiry date). Order counterbalanced.

## 8. Results
- Bottle test: "after" labels were significantly faster for ALL tasks. Only 2 errors (wrong expiry date, both on "before" labels, caught immediately).
- Eye tracking: little gain where the old label was already clear and consistent; big gains where the old label was unclear, small or inconsistent. Significant improvements: time to identify generic names and strengths; fewer fixations for formulations and expiry dates; shorter fixations for dosage strength.
- Conclusion: combining aviation human-factors guidelines with pharmacy guidelines produced a label that is faster to use → likely fewer real-world errors, lower mental workload, better efficiency.

# T13 Cartography and Movement in Space

Sources: (1) Degani, "A Tale of Two Maps: Analysis of the London Underground Diagram" (Ergonomics in Design, July 2013) — the cartography part; (2) Mishler & Neider, "Improving Wayfinding for Older Users With Selective Attention Deficits" (Ergonomics in Design, Jan 2017) — the movement-in-space part.

## Part A — Cartography: a good map "tells little white lies"
- Mark Monmonier (How to Lie with Maps): a good map SUPPRESSES TRUTH to help the user see what needs to be seen. Reality is 3D and too detailed; a map that did not generalise would be useless.
- Key question for designers: how to show a lot of information about a complex system without making it useless? → Abstraction + generalisation.
- Three levels of designing any information display (from Christopher Alexander's "theory of centres"):

| Level | Meaning | In the London map |
| 1. Abstraction | Turning concrete data into simple representational elements | Lines become straight lines; stations become ticks and rings |
| 2. Integration | Joining related elements into coherent structures that show relationships | Grouping interchange stations (station "complexes") as one unit |
| 3. Configuration | Arranging all structures into one whole using an organising principle | Grid, symmetry, strong centre, deformation of space |

(Full London map story is in Topic 17.)

## Part B — Movement in space: wayfinding signs for older users

## Why older people struggle
- With age, spatial memory, route learning, map use, navigation, spotting turning points and forming "mental maps" all decline.
- Result: they avoid driving → lose mobility → poorer physical and mental health. So public spaces must be designed for them.
- Maps are hard for them, so SIGNAGE is the best support. Directional arrows along a virtual route removed the age gap in one study (Head & Isom, 2010).

## The real problem: decline in SELECTIVE ATTENTION

Selective attention = attending to useful information while ignoring the rest. Evidence:
- Distractors and visual clutter hurt older adults much more (even clutter that shouldn't distract; clutter even reduces their visual acuity).
- Older adults narrow their attention under high load.
- Searching for one feature (just a colour) is fine; searching for a CONJUNCTION of features (right colour AND arrow shape) is much slower for them. (Treisman's feature integration theory: single features pop out "pre-attentively"; conjunctions need serial, one-by-one attention.)
- Task maintenance (keeping focus on the goal) is harder.
- Extra guidance helps them MORE than young people: knowing where to look, redundant information, and prior knowledge of features.

## Five design principles (Table 1)

| Principle | Meaning | Example |
| Distinctiveness | Sign easy to pick out from surroundings AND from other kinds of signs (direction vs. room-name signs) | Blue triangular sign on a street of brown buildings with square windows — differs in colour and shape. Or "triple conjunction": borrow window shape + trim colour + storefront font, combined so the sign blends in yet stands out |
| Consistency | Same size, colour, shape, layout and position for related signs; standard images | All wayfinding signs brown with white sans-serif text; same corner of each intersection, same height; man/woman restroom pictogram |
| Simplicity | Only 3–4 units of wayfinding info per sign; no irrelevant logos/ads. Progressive disclosure: general info first, specific near the destination | "West Wing This Way" instead of listing every room in the West Wing |
| Isolation | Place signs where there is little other information | Keep ads and decorations out of the eye-level zone; InfoBand 1 = 47–63 in (approx. 1.2–1.6 m) above floor for reading; InfoBand 2 = 87–118 in (approx. 2.2–3.0 m) for large location signs |
| Reassurance | Extra signs along the route confirm you are on the right path | Sign halfway down a long corridor repeating the upcoming destinations |

- Pairing signs with landmarks helps memory, but the landmark itself may grab attention — the sign must still be distinctive.
- Complex patterns inside the InfoBands should be avoided; complexity OUTSIDE the bands may even help older people focus on the band.
- Too many reassurance signs → clutter; balance is needed.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_010.jpg|Wayfinding sign for older users: distinctive, consistent, simple, reassuring (Mishler & Neider Fig. 1)]
- Example sign (Fig. 1): university building with brownstone, red brick, white trim → rectangular (like bricks), brown (like stone) and white (like trim); standard AIGA symbols; only 3 units of information; reassures "you are in the Smith Building".
- Benefits: better wayfinding for everyone, smaller gap between old and young users, more mobility and independence, less isolation, and older workers can stay competitive.

# T14 Road Safety through Visual Ergonomics

Source: Steyvers & Johnson, "Making Road Safety Visible" (Ergonomics in Design, Winter 2005), Netherlands.

## Background
- The Netherlands: 41,526 sq km, 16 million people, among the most densely populated countries; Dutch drivers cover 315 million km a day → government encourages innovative safety ideas.
- Speed limit signs are placed only where the limit is exceptional. Default limits: 50 km/h in cities, 80 km/h secondary roads, 100 km/h highways, 120 km/h freeways — told to drivers only at international borders. So other ways to communicate safe speed are needed.
- Part of the Dutch "Sustainable Road Safety" initiative: create inherently safe roads and SELF-EXPLANATORY signage — convey safety WITHOUT explicitly telling people to slow down.
- Target: "death roads" — provincial roads with frequent fatal accidents.

## The study
- Symbols of death used to communicate danger: roadside CROSSES (in Holland, accident sites are often marked with a cross with flowers/teddy bears) and MOURNING CARDS (standard cards sent when someone dies).
- Text messages painted on the road: "snel?" ("going fast?") and "haast?" ("in a hurry?").
- 14 slides (incl. a control slide with no signs) showing these signs digitally added to an 80 km/h road. 103 licensed drivers (57 men, 46 women, mean age 37.6) paid about €11.
- Each slide shown once to familiarise, then again while drivers filled a rating sheet: meaning (up to 3 choices: dangerous road, dangerous stretch, dangerous intersection, speeding warning, reduce speed, do not pass, etc.) + 1–5 ratings for comprehensibility, clearness, beauty, pleasantness, emotionality + "would you like to see this on a real road?"

## Results — meaning

| Slide type | Seen as "danger" | Seen as "speed" warning |
| Control (no sign) | 3% | 1% (75% saw "nothing") |
| Mourning cards | 23% | 4% (23% "nothing" — inconsistent) |
| Text only | 27% | 93% |
| Text + painted cross | 43% | 91% |
| Text + roadside crosses | 70% | 82% |
| Crosses only | 52% | 18% |

- No sign was completely unambiguous (true of any un-learned sign).
- "In a hurry?" → mainly a SPEED warning. Adding roadside crosses STRENGTHENED the feeling of DANGER without weakening the speed message. Crosses alone = general danger. Mourning cards were not even recognised → confusing.
- Text slides rated most comprehensible and clear; mourning cards least.

## Results — acceptance
- Even with a graphic designer, symbols were rated negatively for beauty and pleasantness; only text-only was rated more beautiful than the control.
- Almost all slides felt more emotional than the control.
- About 67% did NOT want to see crosses/mourning cards on real roads; only "in a hurry?" was clearly wanted (75% yes).

## "Do they need to like it?"
- Verbal messages were clearest and most accepted, but crosses created a stronger sense of danger → worth testing their effect on actual speed in a simulator/field.
- What pleases drivers is not always what is safest: smooth, good-quality roads make drivers over-estimate safe speed.
- Making drivers slightly uncomfortable (without reducing safety) works: narrowing the perceived lane with a rumble layer of ground rock (sound + vibration) reduced speed and cut accidents by 15–20%. In Haren town, residential streets lined with bricks remind drivers of the low-speed zone (attractive and effective).
- Bad example: a "friendly" sign warning of an on-ramp traffic light had too much small text; drivers merging at 50 km/h had no time to read it → a colleague was rear-ended.
- Conclusion: designing safe road environments needs skill AND art; a message may be correct but rejected emotionally, or pleasant but unclear. Infrastructure and markings can improve compliance without heavy enforcement.

# T15 Password Ergonomics: Designing Better Log-in Pages

Source: Abbas Moallem, "Designing More Effective Log-In and Sign-In Pages" (Ergonomics in Design, Fall 2004).

## Problem
- People log in to many sites daily (bank, broker, email) — maybe 10+ times a day — typing almost the same data (username + password). They often forget them and must recover/reset.
- Each site has different security rules for usernames and passwords, and pages are INCONSISTENT in vocabulary, physical attributes and layout → harder task.

## Method

1. Comparative study of 30 commercial log-in pages.

2. User questionnaire (4 questions): Group 1 = 50 technical consultants (English first language); Group 2 = 23 international conference participants (English not first language).

## Comparative study findings
- Every page had: (1) page/section title, (2) username/ID field, (3) password field, (4) log-in button, (5) forgot password option, (6) forgot username option, (7) help option.
- Titles: Log In variants 63%, Sign In variants 30%; "Login" alone 33%.
- Password label: Password/PASSWORD on 87%; others used PIN or Passcode.
- Button label: 8 different labels; Log In 43%, Sign In 20%.
- Username label: 8+ variations (User Name 30%, User ID 20%, email address, Log-in ID, etc.).
- Physical attributes varied without reason: label ABOVE field 47% vs LEFT of field 53%; plain text 53% vs bold 48%; colon after label 43% vs none 47%.

## User preferences

| Question | Preference |
| Page title | 74% chose Log In variants (51% "Login" as one word); only 16% Sign In |
| Username label | User ID 42% (43% variants); User Name 26% |
| Password label | "Password" — 95% chose it over PIN/Passcode |
| Button label | Mixed: Submit 25%, Go 18%, Enter 16%, Log-in 14%, Sign In 11%, Log In 10% |

- Grammar note: "log in" (two words) is the verb; "login/log-in" is the noun — users still preferred "Login".
- User ID makes more sense than User Name because the ID may be a number or chosen name, not the person's real name.
- Use the SAME word for the page title and the button.

## Recommended standard log-in page (Table 3)

| Object | Label |
| Page title | Log-in |
| User ID field | User ID |
| Password field | Password |
| Button | Log-in |
| Forgot user ID link | Forgot Your User ID? |
| Forgot password link | Forgot Your Password? |
| Help link | Help? |

- Labels left-aligned, plain text (what most sites already do).
- Lesson: STANDARDISATION and CONSISTENCY reduce confusion in a task people repeat many times a day (links to conceptual compatibility, Topic 3).

**Example:** Indian banking apps using "Customer ID", "User ID", "Login ID", "MPIN" for similar things — a standard would reduce errors and lockouts.

# T16 Airport Runway Marking (Better Taxiway Surface Markings)

Source: Andre, Chrysler, Estes, Olmos, Hannon & Andrews, "Better Taxiway Surface Markings → Safer Airports" (Ergonomics in Design, Spring 2005). Sponsored by the FAA.

## The problem: runway incursions
- Taxiing to and from the gate is one of the most complex phases for pilots: many aircraft close together, fast radio communication, checklists, time-pressured navigation → heavy multitasking workload.
- Runway incursion (FAA) = any occurrence on a runway involving an aircraft, vehicle, person or object that creates a collision hazard or loss of separation with an aircraft taking off or landing. Example: crossing the runway hold line without clearance.
- Hold line = where pilots must stop unless cleared onto the runway: solid yellow lines on the taxiway side, dashed lines on the runway side.
- Rare (about 1 per 200,000 surface operations) but serious: 1,480 incursions in the USA 1999–2002; 833 lives lost since 1972.
- Causes: working memory decay, interruption, miscommunication, disorientation.

## Why paint?
- High-tech fixes (head-up displays, runway status lights, moving maps) are expensive and not available everywhere; most US airports don't even have a control tower.
- Paint is CHEAP, SIMPLE and UNIVERSAL — all it needs is a clear windshield. Pilots already focus on the taxiway centreline to keep the nose wheel on it.

## Design goals

1. Eye-height: work for all eye heights (small plane ~8 ft, big jet ~20 ft).

2. Conspicuity: as salient as possible in all weather/visibility.

3. Convey directionality: "stop" from one side, "pass through" from the other.

4. Provide preview: warn that a hold line is coming.

5. Increase awareness, not force action.

6. Intuitive, usable, explicit: easy to learn, use, remember.

7. Unique on several dimensions (not colour alone).

8. Compatible with current markings.

9. Internally consistent (patterns, colours).

10. Preserve essential current elements (yellow = taxiway, white = runway) to minimise retraining.

## Process (a model for any design project)
- Literature review of airport and highway markings + review of European practice.
- Two groups: (1) human factors team (MITRE + consultants) to generate and check usability; (2) FAA, airlines, pilots, air traffic controllers, airport operators, and PAINT CONTRACTORS (who know what is easy to paint) for practical checks.
- 4 workshops over 9 months (from March 2002); about 20 concepts mocked up → 5 survived → simulator test with 36 pilots (general aviation + transport) → reduced to 4 → second simulator test → field tests at T. F. Green Airport, Providence (June 2003) and Boston Logan (Sept 2004) with 224 pilots in total → FAA revised marking standards for large airports.
- Philosophy: include everyone at the START rather than inform them at the end.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_011.jpg|Runway hold line: dashed side = runway, solid side = taxiway (Fig. 1)]

## The three new markings

| Marking | What it is | Why it works |
| Modified taxiway centreline | Dashes on both sides of the yellow centreline for 150 ft before the hold line | Pilots already stare at the centreline → preview warning of a runway ahead; designed for foreshortening; when exiting, its length shows when the tail is clear of the runway. Best for small-plane pilots (eye height 8 ft) |
| Surface-painted holding position signs | Red signs with runway name painted on the ground, on BOTH sides of the centreline at every runway/taxiway intersection | Red appears nowhere else on the taxiway → instantly means "runway"; text right-side-up when approaching, upside-down when leaving (direction); copies existing vertical signs (previous training); placed at the hold line → pilots saw the hold line 20 ft sooner. Very effective for big-jet pilots (eye height 20 ft) |
| Modified runway hold line | Yellow dashes changed to WHITE (white = runway association) and line extended beyond the taxiway edge | Pilots often forgot which side was the runway; white dashes fix that. Longer line = bigger target, visible from side windows when the nose hides the ground (757 nose hides 42 ft ahead; 747 hides 90 ft). Also warns ground vehicles on the shoulders (20% of incursions 1999–2002 involved vehicles) |

## Whole is greater than the sum of parts
- Elements designed to work together as safety nets and give redundancy (if snow covers one, others still show).
- Modified centreline alone: runway detected at 248 ft; painted signs alone: 236 ft; COMBINED: about 20 ft further than the best single cue. Two weak signals together crossed the detection threshold earlier than one strong signal (stopping-rule models; Townsend & Ashby). Pilots: "if unsure of one cue and I see another, together I'm confident".

# T17 Representational Display: the London Metro (Underground) Map

Source: Asaf Degani, "A Tale of Two Maps: Analysis of the London Underground Diagram" (Ergonomics in Design, July 2013).

## Story
- For 50+ years after the network began (1870) the Underground map was GEOGRAPHIC. By the early 1930s London Transport was losing money and passengers found the geographic map hard to understand (Fig. 1, 1932 map by F. H. Stingemore).
- 1931–33: Henry (Harry) Beck, a 29-year-old electrical draughtsman with no graphic design experience, designed an abstract map in his spare time. Initially rejected as "too revolutionary", accepted a year later (1933). Londoners loved it and nicknamed it "the Diagram" because it looked like an electrical wiring diagram.
- Beck transferred copyright to London Transport in exchange for doing all future changes (1937 "gentleman's agreement"). By 1959, after 27 years, London Transport tired of his insistence on control; a PR manager (Harold Hutchinson) made a version and Beck was dropped → long legal dispute; Beck gave up in 1965, died bitter in 1974. Paid perhaps 5–10 guineas (or nothing), yet the Diagram is reproduced 60+ million times a year and became a template for transit maps worldwide; it has accommodated 80 years of new lines.

## Beck's key insight
- The map's main job is to help travellers NAVIGATE INSIDE the network (which line, where to change), not to show true geography. Surface detail is secondary → the network can be shown as an abstract "world".
- Two kinds of station: regular (one line) and interchange (two or more lines).
[FIG:fig/d/ve_012.jpg|Beck's diagrammatic map, 1933 — straight lines, fixed angles, expanded centre (Degani Fig. 2)]

## 1. Abstraction
- Lines straightened and elongated, using only 0° (horizontal), 90° (vertical) and 45°/135° (diagonal) angles → clear, elegant, simple.
- Lines laid out first, stations placed later.
- Regular stations = small square "ticks" (can sit on either side of the line → fits more stations). Interchanges = rings.

## 2. Integration
- Placing interchange stations was the key to success and took most of Beck's effort.
- Station "complexes" (e.g., the "Aldgate Triangle": Liverpool Street, Aldgate, Monument, Bank) must be solved as one integrated unit, not station by station. Beck drew four very different solutions (1965) — rectangular ramps vs. a single ring per station, different escalator symbols (Monument–Bank), white "connector" lines for tunnels.
- Integration = moulding related elements into one coherent structure showing their relationships; each element must support the others — "an abstraction of an abstraction". Needs a keen eye and patience to rework details.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_013.jpg|Four solutions to the 'Aldgate Triangle' complex, 1965 (Degani Fig. 4)]

## 3. Configuration (the whole)
- Strict horizontal/vertical/diagonal orientation.
- Symmetry: a horizontal axis along the Central Line (later also a vertical axis) — imperfect but comfortable "local symmetry".
- A strong centre: the Circle Line forms a "water flask" lying on its side, neck pointing east (Liverpool St, Aldgate, Tower Hill as the cap) → attracts the eye and organises the space; diagonal lines radiating NW and SW create energetic tension.
- Lesson: in 1941–59 Beck removed diagonals for a more rectangular look — this made the pattern LESS clear; diagonals were restored after he left. In 1964 Paul Garbutt even added a diagonal to the District Line (West Ham–Upminster) though the real track is horizontal.
- DEFORMATION of the whole city: central area EXPANDED (space for complex interchanges), outer areas SHRUNK (idea started by Stingemore). Shown by a deformation map with arrows from map position to real position.
- Underlying "relaxed" rectilinear GRID with rhythm (compared to Mondrian's paintings).
[FIG:fig/d/ve_014.jpg|The underlying grid compared with Mondrian's painting (Degani Fig. 7)]
- Kept: real landmarks like the River Thames for orientation.

## 4. Designing for users over time (Victoria Line)
- When the new Victoria Line was being built, Beck proposed first publishing a map already adjusted for it (without the line), then simply inserting it as the only full diagonal in pale pink → the public would see the new line fit into a familiar pattern. Shows sensitivity to users' needs OVER TIME. London Transport ignored it.

## 5. Formal design principles (for any diagram)

1. Choose a skeletal grid of main stations and landmarks (e.g., the Thames) to keep the essential structure.

2. Keep the relative orientation of other stations to these grid points.

3. Keep relative positions (N/S/E/W) of stations on the same line consistent.

4. Use a small set of line orientations (here 3: horizontal, vertical, diagonal).

5. Keep the number of direction changes in each line to a minimum.
- Integration is the hardest to formalise (more artistic); possible tools: clustering stations by shared properties, graph theory, linear programming.
- Wider use: any human–technology interaction can be shown as a network (car climate control, aircraft fuel system, medical devices) → same principles for interfaces; and for adaptive interfaces that reconfigure themselves.

**Example:** Delhi Metro and Mumbai Metro maps follow Beck's style — straight lines, 45° angles, colour-coded lines, interchange rings, expanded centre.

# T18 Poison to the Touch

Source: John W. Senders, "Poison to the Touch" (Ergonomics in Design, Jan 1999) + response by Marilyn Sue Bogner.

## The problem: medication accidents from failing to identify a drug
- Example 1: Potassium chloride (KCl) injected instead of Lasix (furosemide) — usually followed by the patient's death. Both are often prescribed to the same patient: Lasix increases urine → potassium loss → KCl replaces it. KCl must be DILUTED in a saline drip bag and given slowly, never injected directly.
- Example 2: Lidocaine in pre-loaded syringes came in 20% (to be diluted in a drip bag) and 2% (injected directly as a bolus for the heart). Confusion caused 100+ cardiac arrests/deaths (or permanent vegetative state) over 14 years. Withdrawn from the market in 1992 but NOT recalled — an old syringe still killed a patient in December 1995.
- Why it happens: habit and expectation make people "see what they know they are going to see". Relying only on visual labels misses the chance to use INDEPENDENT, PARALLEL sensory channels.

## The idea: make danger tangible
- Use TOUCH (shape and texture) as an alternative channel. As long as the container is held, the warning is felt — NO attention is needed. "If it feels prickly, DO NOT INJECT IT."
- Colour coding is also rarely used (some KCl containers have black caps, but not mandatory).
- Objection 1: 10% of men are colour-blind → but colour could still cut errors by up to 90%.
- Objection 2: not enough distinct shapes/colours → absolute identification is not needed; one rule ("prickly = don't inject") would save many lives.

## History: the 1857 English Poisons Bill (well human-engineered)

Proposed: all poison medicines in QUADRANGULAR BLUE GLASS bottles, labelled in conspicuous capitals WHITE ON BLACK "POISON", with POISON also EMBOSSED in raised letters on all four sides.

1. Black ground = warning colour (today KCl black caps are recommended).

2. Maximum contrast between the word and background (modern multicolour labels often reduce contrast).

3. Embossed on all 4 sides → can't be covered by the hand; survives if the paper label falls off.

4. Rectangular shape → touch cue; blue glass → visual cue (if reserved only for poisons).
- The bill failed when the government fell; the 1868 Pharmacy Act let the Pharmaceutical Society require "bottles distinguishable by touch" for poisonous liniments and lotions.
- Inventors patented: SPINES on caps, protrusions on bottles, "puzzle caps" needing a sequence of turns, upside-down labels, luminous glass, sound alarms. (Fig. 1: the "porcupine strategy".)
- If Parliament could do this 130+ years ago, the FDA can do it now.

## Bogner's response — key points
- Today's homogeneous packaging assumes health workers never suffer stress, fatigue or preoccupation — clearly false.
- Look-alike ampoules (labels differ only by drug name) and look-alike/sound-alike drug names (Hiprex vs Herplex; Nitrospan vs Nitrostat) cause errors, worse in handwriting.
- Adverse drug events: leading cause of medical injury (19.4%) in hospitals; 6.5 per 100 admissions.
- Health workers ACCOMMODATE (adjust, work around) bad products; ordinary consumers COMPLAIN. Example: cameras auto-set film speed, but syringes of very different strengths look identical.
- Successful fixes: Hadassah hospital (Jerusalem) + Technion student designed colour-coded labels for potassium and lidocaine (industry said it was too costly). Australia (AIMS): stimulant/relaxant syringe swaps → hospital bought RED syringes only for relaxants → swaps dropped sharply, cost < 10 cents per syringe.
- Adverse events are SYSTEM problems, not user problems: "changing the user merely changes the actor; the script determines the performance". Change the script (design).
- Health workers should see themselves as CONSUMERS who can demand better design.

# T19 Warnings

Source: Michael S. Wogalter, Joseph L. Seminara & Stuart O. Parsons, "Warnings and Labels: A Summary of Research" — a review of 150+ studies (Ergonomics in Design, Jan 1999), arranged in 24 alphabetical topics. Plus HFED principles (Topic 10).

## Why this paper matters

Warning guidelines no longer need to rest on expert opinion — they are backed by research. Studies sometimes disagree, but general conclusions can be drawn.

## Key findings (alphabetical, as in the paper)

| Factor | What research says |
| Age | Older people: smaller short-term memory → keep warnings brief and direct; difficulty linking symbols; need larger print (large-print medicine labels gave better knowledge). Warnings worked less on high-school than middle-school students |
| Auditory warnings | Omnidirectional and attention-grabbing; tones, auditory icons ("earcons"), voice. Urgency changes with pulse rate etc. Best when the sound relates to the situation (car-skid sound for braking). Voice warnings strongly increase compliance; long complex messages better shown visually; FALSE ALARMS cause annoyance |
| Believability | Warnings that state CONSEQUENCES are more believable; a respected source (FDA, medical association) adds weight |
| Border | Wide, colourful, jagged borders seem more effective; border width ↑ = urgency ↑; raised (tactile) border improved noticing, comprehension, recall; thick red or yellow/black striped borders attract examination |
| Brevity | Short, direct is better — but must still include needed specifics; too many minor warnings DILUTE perceived risk; people like explicit warnings of moderate length |
| Colour | Colour attracts attention if it contrasts with surroundings. RED = highest hazard; orange and yellow = less (little difference between them); red gives faster detection and more compliance. Holds for children (8+) and Spanish speakers |
| Compliance | The real test = actual safe behaviour. Warnings generally increase safe behaviour (meta-analysis of 15 studies). Helped by: pictorials, the person's name, location near the hazard, uncluttered surroundings, social influence (others complying), redundancy, video modelling, and providing protective equipment. Repeated FALSE warnings make people ignore them (drivers sped up again) |
| Consequence information | One of the 4 essential statements; tells people WHY to comply and motivates them. Risk must cross a threshold before people make an effort |
| Conspicuity | First requirement: getting noticed. Use larger/bolder print, contrast, highlighting, flashing. Relevance helps (heavy drinkers noticed alcohol warnings more) |
| Consumer buying | Warnings do NOT reduce buying; they may even make a brand look safer |
| Culture / ethnicity | Use language-independent pictorials in public places (airports, stations); test across cultures |
| Explicitness | Specific hazard, consequence and instructions → more perceived hazard and caution; very threatening warnings may not be posted |
| Design / layout | Bullet/outline format beats paragraphs; pictorials and colour aid noticing and understanding; shape has a small effect; redundant features and multiple senses help; fold-out labels/tags for small containers; container SHAPE can signal hazard. Lay people often can't tell a good warning from a bad one until they see a good one |
| Familiarity | The more familiar the product, the LESS people notice warnings and comply; previous injury makes people more careful |
| Gender | Mostly no difference; where found, women look for, read and comply more |
| Hazardousness | People judge risk mainly by SEVERITY of injury, not probability; higher perceived hazard → more noticing, reading, complying. People overestimate rare risks and underestimate common ones |
| Interactive warnings | Warnings that physically block the task (must be moved to use the product) give more noticing, recall and compliance |
| Placement | One of the most important factors: put warnings NEAR the hazard in time and space; clutter increases search time and reduces compliance; enhanced-area labels help; order matters (warnings before or embedded in instructions often better); prioritise by importance, severity and likelihood |
| Print | Large, bold, simple sans-serif (Helvetica) for big signs; serif (Times) OK for small labels; stroke not too wide or compressed |
| Recall | Helped by repetition, relevance, pictorials and injury statistics |
| Responsibility | If a warning is missing/poor and the hazard is hidden, jurors blame the manufacturer more |
| Signal words | ANSI words: DANGER (highest) > WARNING > CAUTION > NOTICE (lowest). WARNING vs CAUTION is unclear. DEADLY and POISON rate even higher than DANGER. Same order for children, elderly, non-native speakers |
| Symbols / pictorials | Help non-readers, children, other languages; attract attention; aid memory. Standards: ISO 7001 = 67% correct comprehension; ANSI Z535 = 85% correct and ≤5% "opposite meaning" errors. Many symbols fail. Use simple, bold, continuous figures (gestalt); test open-ended and in context. Circle-slash = prohibition (don't hide the picture); skull-and-crossbones raises perceived hazard; the safety alert symbol (! in a triangle) signals a warning but doesn't raise hazard. Older people understand symbols less |
| Wording | Four elements: (1) SIGNAL WORD, (2) HAZARD, (3) CONSEQUENCES, (4) INSTRUCTIONS to avoid it. "GASOLINE – NO SMOKING" (hazard + instruction) rated better than either alone. It is easier to match existing knowledge than teach new |

## Model warning (apply the 4 elements)

| Element | Example |
| Signal word | ⚠ DANGER |
| Hazard | High voltage |
| Consequence | Contact will cause severe burns or death |
| Instruction | Keep out. Only authorised staff |

## Future research needed

Real-world compliance studies; virtual reality to test risky situations; effects of alcohol, drugs, fatigue, stress and personality ("risk takers"); wider populations (children, elderly, non-native); long-term studies.

# T20 Road Safety and Visual Ergonomics: Truck "Wide Turn" Warning Signs

Source: David M. Aurelio & Todd M. Newman, "Better Warning Signs Can Help Prevent Cars and Tractor-Trailer Trucks from Colliding" (Ergonomics in Design, April 1997). File name in the folder: "Graphic turn NEW.pdf".

## Problem
- Trucks and cars differ in size, weight and acceleration. Driving behind and to the RIGHT of a tractor-trailer is dangerous: when the truck makes a WIDE RIGHT TURN it can hit a car that is too close.
- Trucks carry rear signs like "Caution – Wide Turns", but these signs are VOLUNTARY and NOT REGULATED (NHTSA regulates only reflective, self-illuminated or hazardous-cargo signs) → huge variety.

## Method
- Photographed 25 signs on trucks parked at a truck stop on Interstate 95 near Boston over two weeks (safer than photographing while driving). Truck drivers were suspicious — they were assured no licence plates would be shown.
- Grouped signs: text only, top view, side view, horizontal, vertical, showing an accident, showing a near-accident, miscellaneous.
- Five human factors researchers judged them from professional knowledge (no guidelines in hand) and picked the best sign or the best elements.

## How the 25 signs varied

| Characteristic | Variations |
| Colours | Two or three colours; yellow, red, black, white, blue |
| Point of view | Top view, oblique, or side (perspective) view of car and truck |
| Elements | Text only, or text + diagram |
| Signal word | CAUTION, WARNING, or none |
| Orientation | Horizontal or vertical |
| Sketch content | Accident scene, conditions leading to an accident, safe scene |
| Information | Hazard only; signal word + hazard; signal word + hazard + instructions |

- Sizes about 45 × 45 cm to 45 × 60 cm. All said "Wide Turns" or "Wide Right Turns".
- Most interesting: a three-colour sign showing what a car driver sees through the windshield just before hitting the truck, with tilted lettering — yellow + white text on red.

## Evaluation against warning guidelines
- A warning should contain: signal word, hazard, consequences, instructions (Sanders & McCormick); ANSI Z535.2: signal word, text message, pictorial.
- Most signs had only signal word + hazard ("Caution – Wide Turns"). NONE described the consequence (collision) in words, though some drew it.
- Instructions were weak: "Please Give-Way" is vague; "Do Not Pass on Right Side" doesn't cover a car already beside the turning truck.
- Pictorial can show either the CONSEQUENCE (e.g., "No Diving!" sign showing a head hitting the pool bottom) or the INSTRUCTION (mother watching a child for "Watch Children") — choose the level of "morbidity" to suit the hazard and audience.

## Improved sign (Fig. 5)
[FIG:fig/d/ve_015.jpg|Preliminary improved wide-turn warning sign (Aurelio & Newman Fig. 5)]
- Full text would be: "Caution – This vehicle makes wide right turns. You can become injured if you collide with the truck. Do not pass on right side. Please yield the right of way to the truck." — too long for a moving reader, so trimmed to: "CAUTION — Wide right turns. Yield the right of way to truck."
- Signal word in its own field, centred at the top. Simplified perspective side-view sketch of a car colliding with the truck's right side, with a STARBURST at the collision point.
- Black text, upper + lowercase, LEFT-justified; white or yellow background for contrast against the trailer.
- Needs further testing. Conclusion: no regulations exist, but owners voluntarily warn the public; better signs can reduce truck–car crashes.

**Example (India):** "Horn OK Please", "Keep Distance" painted on trucks — informal, inconsistent, rarely state the hazard or consequence; a standard "blind spot / wide turn" sign would follow the same principles.

# T21 Parking Enforcement through Visual Ergonomics

Source: "Human Factors in Parking Enforcement" (Ergonomics in Design, Spring 2000) — the Chicago parking ticket redesign, written by the official who led it.

## The problem (Chicago, 1980s)
- People ignored parking rules; records were chaotic; no credible enforcement → cars in loading zones, delivery trucks double-parked, traffic jams, pollution trouble with the EPA.
- Only about 10% of tickets paid; 17 million unpaid tickets worth $420 million.
- Mayor Richard M. Daley (elected 1989) wanted a visible success; the author (a lawyer from the prosecutor's office) got 10 months (Nov 1989 – Sept 1990) to write an ordinance, design the system, hire a vendor, build neighbourhood service centres, design tickets and forms, convert 17 million records, and train police and the public.

## Results
- Went live on time and within budget. In one year: same-year payment rate 10% → 65%; annual revenue $65 million; cost to taxpayers down about $5 million.
- Finalist (25 of 1,900) for the Harvard/Ford Foundation Innovations award; Computerworld/Smithsonian award; praised in Fortune and the Chicago Tribune.
- Only about 7% of tickets contested; about ¾ of those by mail → fewer visits to centres. Almost ZERO phone calls from confused motorists.

## Design process

## 1. "Think systems" — image-based design
- Flow diagrams (information paths) and Venn diagrams (what must coincide in time/place) helped them think from scratch, not just make the old system faster.
- Insight: adjudication needs matching INFORMATION, not people and paper → tickets scanned, images stored on optical disk; hearing officer sees ticket image, plate history and meter-repair database in separate windows and keys "liable/not liable" (5 legal reasons) → decision letter generated in plain English. Mail-in cases queued as electronic folders; any free officer takes the oldest. This made neighbourhood centres cost-effective.

## 2. The ticket = the item that touches every part of the system
- 12,000 police officers wrote about 3 million tickets/year; 40 parking aides wrote another 1 million. Tickets had to be printed, distributed, written, survive sun and rain on windshields, explain how to pay or dispute, be scanned and keyed, and their return envelopes sorted and posted.
- "Ticket life cycle" → SIMULTANEOUS (concurrent) design team of about a dozen: users, vendors for printing, scanning, envelope-opening, cashiering, BEAT police officers and parking aides (not just a training bulletin), and a graphic designer (Sue Brodarick) for type size, font, layout.
- Constraints discussed at one table: legal elements; white space around the OCR number for scanners; margins for envelope-slitting machines; police demand — ticket book must fit in a rear trouser pocket! One attractive layout needed two passes on the press (double cost) → rejected.

## 3. Design decisions from real use
- Officers said most tickets are for a few offences → database showed 9 violations = about 80% of tickets → CHECK-OFF BOXES.
- First mock-up put check boxes on the left and handwriting on the right — then they realised tickets are written by RIGHT-HANDED people STANDING in the street → the left side is better supported → handwritten info moved to the LEFT → more legible writing, cheaper carbon paper.
- Writing on white paper (legible); ticket placed face-down to protect the writing and show the BRIGHT ORANGE back (mayor chose the colour) — police wouldn't follow a rule, so a small ADHESIVE STRIP made it attachable ONLY orange-side up (a forcing design). Cheaper paper possible; a few cents × 4 million tickets a year.
- Handheld computers for aides: licence plate entered first (their preference); Illinois as default state; current street as default; type 3 letters → street menu; violation list with most frequent first; keys usable in rain and with gloves.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_016.jpg|The redesigned Chicago violation notice (parking ticket), 1990]

## 4. Testing ticket instructions (rough-and-ready user testing)
- Groups: 5 new high-school drivers, city secretaries, tradespeople. Task: "Suppose you found this ticket on your car. What are your choices?"
- Every odd interpretation → find the words causing it → rewrite. 10 days of iterations with new volunteers until no wrong interpretations remained. ("Prompted explanation", like the puppeteer technique.)
- Goal: give motorists the right MENTAL MODEL of the new system (the old model was "ignore it, or go to court and get it dismissed or fixed").
- Users' mental model differed from the law: law = 2 options (pay, or dispute → by mail / in person); people saw 3 options: (a) pay by cheque, (b) mail an explanation with evidence, (c) request a hearing. The team ADOPTED the users' model → 3 check-off boxes next to the return address, which also let staff sort mail before opening.
- Some people will always "just call the hotline" — accept an irreducible number.

## Lessons for designers
- Focus on the business application: present good design as reducing future risk and cost, not "meeting HF requirements". The contractor (paid per ticket, also running the hotline and notices) profited most when people paid or disputed correctly without calling — aligned incentives.
- Use business language: design is an INVESTMENT; "test marketing" instead of "usability testing"; customer focus.
- Real choice is new design vs. the CURRENT one, not vs. nothing (Type II errors).
- Simultaneous engineering: involve every stage of the product life cycle (make, ship, use, repair) at the start → avoid expensive redesign.
- Human factors principles are intuitive enough for police, contractors and business people to use; user involvement creates buy-in.

# T22 Jugglery with Colour (Illusion, Magic and Tricky Design)

Source: Linda C. Williams, "Using Magic to Throw Light on Tricky Healthcare Systems: Patient Safety Problem Solving" (Human Factors & Ergonomics in Manufacturing, 2012). File: "Ergonomics and Illusion-1.pdf". ("Jugglery" = trickery/magic; the paper shows how colour, packaging and display "tricks" fool the eye.)

## Core idea
- "The essence of magic is fundamental deception" (Blackstone). On stage, illusion is fun because we KNOW we are being deceived.
- In healthcare, illusions are INADVERTENT — unintended "magic" hidden in devices, software, packaging and buildings. Designers (engineers, programmers, architects) don't see it, yet the CLINICIAN is blamed when the illusion harms a patient.
- Magicians exploit the same limits of attention, awareness, trust and perception that human factors (HFE) studies. Good design uses these principles to DIRECT correct use; magic uses them to MISDIRECT.
- Culture problem: healthcare admires experts who master tricky devices, so trickiness is accepted. Without HFE, each clinician must become an expert on every device — a lonely, stressful, often impossible vigilance.

## Good design is the opposite of trickery (Nielsen, Gosbee)

Simple, natural dialogue; minimise memory load (recognition, not recall); user control and freedom; flexibility and efficiency; FEEDBACK; help recognise, diagnose and recover from errors; affordances that guide correct use; progressive detail; readable labels and warnings; DON'T LIE TO THE USER.

## The tricks and their healthcare versions

| Magic trick | How it fools us | Healthcare illusion | Lesson |   |
| Card trick (pick a card — it "disappears") | Five different cards are shown afterwards; we don't keep the whole set in mind | (a) Chest drains: similar devices, colours and names but different use — one needs water, another doesn't; long, confusing instructions. (b) Lab report: a 7-month-old baby's report left out neutrophils because the value was 0% → doctor assumed normal; baby was defenceless against infection | Show ALL values, even zero (hospital changed report format). Humans are "pattern-mad supposing machines" — the mind fills gaps with what it expects |   |
| "It's a GAG" | Same substance, many forms | Sodium hyaluronate: one for EYE surgery, one for ORTHOPAEDIC use — same generic name, same concentration, both refrigerated, from two countries, BOTH in similar WHITE-AND-BLUE packaging; only viscosity differs | Surgeons shouldn't have to test viscosity — packaging COLOUR and labels must reveal the difference |   |
| Magic rope (3 unequal ropes become equal) | Different things appear the same | Look-alike/sound-alike drug names: Cerebyx (fosphenytoin, anticonvulsant), Celebrex (celecoxib, anti-inflammatory), Celexa (citalopram, antidepressant) — confused when ordering, dispensing, giving. ISMP keeps an 8-page list | TALL MAN LETTERING highlights differences: CeleBREX vs CeleXA |   |
| Disappearing ball (is it there or not?) | Status is uncertain | Therac-25 radiation machine: no feedback, technologist pressed the button repeatedly → overdoses. Ventilator stuck in pre-check mode because a software update deleted "press ← | to start" | Clear STATUS and PROGRESS indicators and feedback |
| Frog-and-penny (penny looks near the top of a dome, is really at the bottom) | Optical illusion | Laparoscopic bile-duct surgery: pulling the gallbladder rearranges anatomy so the common bile duct LOOKS like the cystic duct; surgeons deliberately cut the wrong duct (97% of injuries) | "Surgeons see what they believe" — mental model shapes perception |   |

## Conclusion
- Learning simple magic tricks is an educational way to show health workers their own perceptual limits and the value of HFE tools (usability testing, heuristic evaluation, observation).
- If a device is "tricky", it is a hazard; the complexity of the fix justifies hiring HFE experts.

Link to colour: colour and packaging similarity (white–blue boxes), colour coding of tubes and syringes (Topic 18 red syringes) can either CREATE illusions or DISPEL them.

# T23 Visual Ergonomics in Election (Voting)

Source: Gable, Chen, Darling, McGlynn, Kazi, Preusse, Yoo & Schaeffer, "Heuristic-Driven Recommendations for Improving U.S. Voting" (Ergonomics in Design, 2016) — winning entry of the HFES 2014 "Voting System of Tomorrow" competition.

## Problems
- US voter turnout ranks 31st of 34 developed countries; partly due to inefficient polling stations and long waits.
- 2000 presidential election: the confusing BUTTERFLY BALLOT in Florida (candidate names on both sides, punch holes in the middle) caused about 50,000 votes to be discarded; machines didn't tell voters their vote was invalid.
- Even college students made at least one error on 11% of ballots in an experiment; ballot design affects wait times.
- Method: interviews, literature review, brainstorming → a HEURISTIC-driven prototype website (needs data-driven testing later).
[FIG:fig/d/ve_017.jpg|The 2000 Florida 'butterfly ballot' — about 50,000 votes discarded (Gable et al. Fig. 1)]

## Recommendations (with the HF/E principle in brackets)

## A. Efficient voting-related processes

| Feature | What | Principle |
| One-stop shop | One website for registering, moving paperwork, contacting officials, learning about contests, checking eligibility/registration, finding polling place, practising mock ballots (not online voting itself — security) | Lower information-access cost |
| Voter accounts | Store voter data so it isn't re-entered | Recognition rather than recall |
| Appointment option | Book a time slot with priority lines/booths; spreads voters (most vote 7–8 am or 5–7 pm) | Closure; prediction aiding |

## B. Ballot design

| Feature | What | Principle |
| Progress meter | Shows votes marked and items remaining | Visibility of system status |
| Final check | Summary screen before submitting | Error prevention and correction |
| Receipt printing | Printed confirmation of votes | Informative feedback |
| Feedback | Green check marks for success; error messages that SAY what to do ("You selected 3, limit is 2 — remove one") | Informative feedback (screens feel less permanent than paper) |
| Reversibility | Change any earlier vote before casting | Error correction / undo |
| Accessibility | Adjustable text size and language; audio for low vision; screen-reader friendly tab order; tactile redundant coding | Universal design (helps everyone) |
| Consistent layout | Same layout on every screen (contest name, candidates, progress, options in fixed places) | Consistency |
| Practice ballot | Electronic practice ballot matching the real one | Consistency between steps |
| Randomise candidate order | People read in an "F-pattern", so first-listed candidates get extra votes (ballot order effect) → random order per voter (but keep it fixed if the voter returns) | Fairness |

# T25 Wayfinding in Long-Term Care (Wayfinding for Older Adults)

Source: Tonya L. Watson, "Wayfinding" (Ergonomics in Design, Winter 2001). Related: Topic 13 (Mishler & Neider).

## Bob's story (shows every wayfinding failure)

Bob drives to a new County Courthouse: circles the block to find the parking entrance → can't see on entering the dark garage (eyes must dark-adapt) → only "More Parking" and "Exit" signs; worries he'll forget where he parked → walks to a service elevator by mistake; public elevator marked only on the far side → elevator buttons "LL1, LL2, 1–12" — doesn't know LL = lower level, not lobby → information-desk staff speaks too fast and softly → tiny "You Are Here" map on a far wall, has to turn his head sideways to match it with the building → wrong elevator bank → 10th floor is a sea of grey cubicles; asks a worker who angrily snaps at him → glossy room-number plates with glare → walks almost the whole floor to find Room 1022B → realises he left the papers in the car!

## What is wayfinding?
- The ability to navigate effectively through an environment — what people SEE, THINK and DO to get from one place to another. It defines our connection with the environment. (Paper focuses on indoor wayfinding and older adults.)
- Functions involved: sensing and perceiving, visual functioning, perceptual functioning, information processing, attending, reasoning/decision making/planning, remembering, spatial processing, moving/locomoting, vestibular (balance) functioning.
- Three ability groups: Sensory-perceptual (vision, hearing); Cognitive (attention, reasoning, memory, spatial processing); Movement control/motor.
- Problems arise whenever the person's abilities are exceeded. "Without signs, wayfinding is reduced to a guessing game."

## Design guidelines by ability (table)

| Ability | Guidelines |
| Vision | Exterior signs facing the driver, readable day and night from the car; black on white or yellow; bright, non-reflective; outside letters at least 4–5 in (10–13 cm); inside 5/8–2 in (1.6–5.1 cm); light walls, good lighting; transition lighting between outdoors and indoors |
| Attention | Short messages, maximum 7 words |
| Memory | Signs visible from all parking spaces with level and space number; important signs at intervals no farther than sight of the previous one |
| Cognition/comprehension | Positive wording; elevator buttons "Sub 1" for below ground and "Lobby" for the lobby; staff trained to speak at the right speed and volume; printed maps the desk staff can draw on and give away |
| Orientation | Light and colour to highlight public destinations; mark destinations on all sides and distinguish multiple elevator banks; signs for pedestrians and vehicles to elevators/stairs; legible, accessible You-Are-Here maps with N/S/E/W, an inset showing the whole complex, aligned with the building ("forward is up"), with landmarks; signs at every decision point; floor number and room directions opposite the elevator; trained information staff, not random workers; logical room numbering |
| Safety | Emergency call devices on each floor; extra lighting in risky areas |

## Why older adults struggle more
- Vision: acuity declines fast from 50 to 90; useful field of view (UFOV — what one glance captures) shrinks greatly from 22 to 75; visual search gets harder with more items; slower dark/light adaptation; more affected by glare and slower to recover; poorer depth perception (30–80 yrs); need more contrast; trouble with blue-green colours.
- Hearing: presbycusis (high-frequency hearing loss) is almost inevitable; speech perception declines from about 30.
- Memory: working memory declines → can't hold several directions; spatial memory (remembering locations) is weaker.
- Reasoning: weaker with abstract or complex situations → complex building = more difficulty.
- Spatial abilities: peak in mid-life then decline; harder to mentally rotate a map or keep orientation.
- Processing speed: slower, especially for complex tasks → give them time.
- Movement: slower to start, move and stop; less force control; walking speed drops 10–20% per decade after 60; attention spent on walking safely leaves less for wayfinding.

## Environmental supports for older people

Sensory-perceptual: 1. more light; 2. transition areas for adaptation; 3. higher contrast on signs and numbers; 4. control glare; 5. avoid blue-green discriminations; 6. bigger print and objects; 7. non-physiological depth cues (perspective, texture); 8. contrasting trim/baseboards to show wall–door–floor edges; 9. make critical items conspicuous (size, contrast, colour, motion); 10. control background noise; 11. avoid sounds above 3000 Hz; 12. louder important sounds.

Cognitive: 1. clear, paced, redundant, context-rich verbal directions with visual cues; 2. many external memory aids (signs, maps); 3. time and space to orient; 4. reduce mental map manipulation; 5. orientation signs at each entrance; 6. cardinal directions on all maps; 7. outside views.

Movement: 1. seating at intervals for rest and orientation; 2. wide corridors; 3. good glare-free stair lighting with clear tread/riser contrast; 4. handrails extended beyond first and last steps; 5. fixed rugs; 6. avoid thick cushioned floors; 7. slow-closing elevator doors.
- A good wayfinding system is designed in advance as a SYSTEM, not "a few signs here and there". "Know thy user." Good design is "equal opportunity" — it helps all ages.

**Example (India):** a government hospital where OPD, labs and wards are spread across blocks — colour-coded floor lines, Hindi/English/regional signs with pictograms, "You are here" maps at every entrance, seating along long corridors (links to the Hospital Navigation Kit project).

# T24 Hospital Information

Four papers are grouped here because they all deal with how information is shown and used in hospitals: (A) Emergency Department tracking board, (B) urine bags, (C) checklists, (D) medication instruction sheets.

## A. "Tracking Care in the Emergency Department" — ED LaunchPoint (Green, Buckley & Scott, Ergonomics in Design, Oct 2015; honourable mention, 2013 Stanley H. Caplan User-Centered Product Design Award)

## Problem
- Electronic medical records (EMRs) should help clinicians, but in complex places like the Emergency Department (ED) they are often felt as an OBSTACLE. ED tasks are many, simultaneous, fast, technically difficult and often unclear about next steps.
- A complex environment = many items, high interdependence, frequent and fast changes, low predictability.
- Cerner Corporation built ED LaunchPoint, a software TRACKING BOARD, with a cross-functional team (clinical strategists, business analysts, software architects, interaction designers, usability researchers).

## User-centred design (UCD) process

Understand users → create a vision → iterative design + usability testing → validate and deliver.
- Early research: site visits with 72 ED physicians in the USA, Canada, UK and Australia (community hospitals to large networks); interviews, usage metrics, observation; measured steps per task, errors, comments, pain points.
- Information needs found: nursing triage summary + key history (the "patient story": drug use, recent visits, infections); quick access to up to 4 charts; order progress and expected result times; department-specific alert thresholds for length of stay.

| Pain point | Example |
| Status icons not actionable | New lab result indicator couldn't be marked as seen → more clicks |
| Repetitive actions took many clicks | Select patient → right-click tree menu or toolbar |
| Icons hard to decipher | Too many, inconsistent, badly placed — doctors TAPED an icon key to the monitor |
| Cluttered board | Columns for every user, not organised by workflow → clutter and slow performance |
| Waiting | Often >10 s spinner while patient data loaded |

- Vision: site maps, storyboards, sketches; iterative formative tests with 5–7 clinicians (on site and remote from Kansas City), 30+ findings.

## Four redesigned features

| Feature | Change through iterations | Final design |
| Orders (labs, medication, imaging, ECG) | Many confusing icons → combined by category using data-visualisation and task priority; first ring-around-icon for duration not understood | One icon per order category + a progress indicator; different degrees of COLOUR show the stage and how overdue it is |

## B. "Evaluation of Changes to Urine Bags Designed to Improve Visual Ergonomics and Reduce Human Error" (Sihombing et al., Ergonomics in Design, 2026; Hasan Sadikin Hospital, Indonesia)
- Medical error = failure of a planned action or use of a wrong plan (Institute of Medicine). Unsafe care is among WHO's top 10 causes of death and disability. Workload, fatigue and distraction contribute; constant switching between visual tasks causes visual fatigue.
- In ICUs, urine output monitoring detects acute kidney injury and guides fluid balance — must be FAST and ACCURATE. Automatic urinometers are costly, so manual bags are used; their small, low-contrast markings are hard to read under time pressure or from a distance.
- Modified bag (same 2600 mL capacity): (1) FLOAT devices (15 mm polyethylene floats in 3 transparent guide channels) to show the fluid level; (2) ENLARGED high-contrast scale markings; (3) COLOUR-CODED bands (matte ink).
- Method: 34 ICU general practitioners (20/20 vision, mean age 30); bag hung 15 cm above floor; random volumes (e.g., 65, 125, 275 mL); read from 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 m; measured time, error, maximum reliable distance, comfort (0–5 scale).
[FIG:fig/d/ve_022.jpg|Modified urine bag (A) with floats, big scale and colour bands vs standard bag (B)]
- Results: estimation error significantly lower with the modified bag at 7–2 m (e.g., at 5 m: 11.8 mL vs 2.4 mL); both perfect at 1 m. Reliable viewing distance increased by a median 2 m (mean about 5.0 m → 6.4 m). Time was shorter at all distances (0.4–1.4 s) but significant only at 6 m. Comfort: modified bag median 4/5; water-level visibility 4 vs 3 for the standard bag.
- Why it matters: 6 m = reading from the nursing station or foot of the bed → fewer steps, less contact (infection control), multi-patient monitoring. At short distances both bags are easy (ceiling effect); around 6 m the standard bag hits its perceptual limit.
- Lesson: SMALL, LOW-COST visual changes (salience, size, colour, a moving indicator) reduce error.

## C. "Checklists: A Review of Their Origins, Benefits, and Current Uses as a Cognitive Aid in Medicine" (Chaparro, Keebler, Lazzara & Diamond, Ergonomics in Design, April 2019)
- Cognitive aids (mnemonics, posters, pocket cards, calculators, checklists) extend memory, attention and thinking. 88.8% of hospital quality leaders said their hospitals use checklists.
- Origin: 1935 crash of Boeing Model 299 (B-17 prototype) — pilots forgot to release the "gust lock" on the elevators; 2 of 5 died. The plane was too complex to remember every step → checklists → now mandatory in aviation (FAA, EASA).
- Definition (Hales et al.): a list of action items arranged consistently so the evaluator can record presence or absence of each item.
- Evidence: Johns Hopkins 5-step central-line checklist (wash hands; chlorhexidine skin prep; sterile drapes; sterile mask, gown, gloves; sterile dressing) → bloodstream infections 11% → 0%; 100+ Michigan ICUs → 66% fewer. WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (19 items in 3 phases: before anaesthesia, "time out" before incision, "sign out" before closing) → complications in 8 hospitals 11% → 7%.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_023.jpg|WHO Surgical Safety Checklist — before anaesthesia, time out, sign out]

| Types (Scriven) | Types by function (Higgins & Boorman) |
| Sequential (fixed order) | Procedural (order matters) |
| Laundry list (no order) | Preparation (items gathered, order less important) |
| Iterative (repeated, e.g., nuclear plants) | Problem-solving (decision tree, e.g., EMTs) |
| Diagnostic (decision aid) | Prevention (targets known error causes) |
| Criteria of merit (evaluating trainees) |   |

- Execution: READ-DO (read each item then do it, like a recipe) vs DO-CONFIRM (do from memory, then confirm nothing was missed — adds redundancy).
- Benefits: (1) memory aid; (2) reduces effects of fatigue, stress, distraction; (3) standardises tasks across people; (4) ensures best practice; (5) promotes team communication.
- Barriers: fear of losing independence; imposed by administration; ignores local situations; adds complexity; seen as a sign of weakness.
- Solutions: involve users in design (ownership); fit the checklist into the wider system; revise over time; check a checklist is the right tool; share mishap stories (crew resource management) — using a checklist is "do no harm", not lack of skill (Gawande).

## D. "Making Medication Instructions Usable" (Klein & Isaacson, Ergonomics in Design, Spring 2003)
- Real dilemmas: give the baby's dose if there's a rash? "Take with food" — does a cookie count? Did anyone check my other medicines? → effective medicines can become POISONS if instructions aren't understood. 2.5 billion prescriptions filled in 1998; compliance depends on UNDERSTANDING.
- Problems in commercial information sheets: PHYSICAL (9-pt type, dot-matrix low contrast, multiple fonts, lots of capitals, tight line spacing → lateral masking, fully justified text); LANGUAGE (12th-grade "fairly difficult" readability, 15.7-word average sentences, some 35+ words, passive voice, jargon like "drug-induced photosensitivity"); ORGANISATION (prose paragraphs, few bullets/boxes, hard to find "Where do I store this?" or "Can I have a beer?").
- Redesigned "human-factored" sheet: 14-pt type; laser print for contrast; flush-left ragged-right; mostly lowercase with a few capitals; short sentences, simple words, active and positive statements (9th-grade "plain English"); task-centred order (name, then purpose — the order users expect); bold headings; side effects in bulleted "minor" and "serious" boxes; spacing to show structure.
- Results: 62 college students — faster review (77 s vs 119 s) and test (122 vs 151 s), more correct answers (8.1 vs 7.5), 90% preferred new sheet. 41 older adults (58–87) — faster reading (85 vs 119 s), easier to remember/locate/follow, 88% preferred. 39 pharmacy customers — new sheet better on all 9 items.
- Remaining errors: many still said "bathroom cabinet" for storage; missed alcohol interactions; discounted rashes; answered from habit rather than the sheet → still more work needed (video, internet, self-management support for diabetes etc.).

# T26 Litigation and Human Factors (Forensic Human Factors)

Sources: (A) Kenneth Nemire, "Cognitive Human Factors in Litigation" (Ergonomics in Design, Jan 2011); (B) Fairbrother, Readdick & Shea, "A Forensic Investigation of a Portable Crib Collapse" (Ergonomics in Design, Winter 2008).

## A. Cognitive human factors in litigation
- Seemingly simple acts go terribly wrong (buying fish food → broken ankle). Human factors experts explain these in court using the human information-processing model: detection → perception → attention → memory → decision making → action.
- Falls and motor-vehicle accidents are among the commonest causes of injury and lawsuits. Product-liability cases ask about foreseeable use and misuse and whether warnings were effective.

| Case | Stage | What happened | Expert's analysis |
| 1. Fish food | Sensation & perception | Robert followed a store employee down an aisle and slipped on water → fractured ankle, several surgeries; store said he should have seen it | When walking, gaze is mostly ahead with a floor glance about every 10 ft; following someone closely, a glance shows their legs/feet, not the floor. Water on tile had luminance contrast of only 0.03 → very hard to see unless pointed out. Settled |
| 2. Truck and pedestrian | Attention | Michelle (high-school student, bright orange shirt) killed by a pickup on a clear day; driver George: "I didn't see her" | No visual obstruction; truck 120 ft away at 40 mph when she stepped off 2 s before impact — enough time to brake. | 3. Chair fire | Decision making | Jennifer found a hand-sized fire, woke husband, went to the outside extinguisher cabinet — the mallet to break the glass was missing; went to her parents for help; flashover killed husband and two children; defence blamed her | NFPA guidelines (alert people, call fire dept, small contained fire, safe escape route) — she followed most; perceived competence lowers perceived risk; stress narrows cues and fixes people on their first plan; hindsight bias; asking parents was natural. | 4. - Lesson: applying cognitive ergonomics research explains injuries and helps resolve cases; good design and maintenance could have prevented them.

## B. Forensic investigation of a portable crib collapse
- 1998: 17-month-old Danny Keysar died when the hinged top railings of a Playskool Trav'l Lite portable crib folded into a V and trapped his neck. The crib: two plastic end pieces, folding floor, folding top rails, mesh sides; KNOBS at the corners rotate the rails to lock the hinge (a click was reported in the final design but not found; decals showed correct orientation).
- Aim: not "what DID happen" but "what COULD have happened" — could a child like Danny rotate the rails?
- THREE-FACTOR APPROACH (Schmidt & Wrisberg): the PERFORMER (users), the TASK (uses), the ENVIRONMENT (usage contexts).
- Users: adults buy and set up the crib, but the CHILD is also a user — with goals opposite to the adult's (escape, explore).
- Uses: meant for supervised play/nap, but a reasonably foreseeable "off-label" use is UNSUPERVISED confinement. US law (CPSC 1997): a product is defective if it ignores reasonably foreseeable use or misuse. Products afford certain uses (Gibson) — like people walking on grass instead of the path or using a screwdriver as a pry bar.
- Context: unsupervised → the child explores escape: leg over the rail, climbing mesh, shaking, lifting, rotating rails and knobs.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_025.jpg|How the collapsing rail trapped a child's neck (Fairbrother et al. Fig. 2)]
- Phase 1 Event analysis → Phase 2 Torque measurement → Phase 3 Video observation.
- Torque: at least 5.00 in-lb rotated Knob 1; 6.00 in-lb rotated the first railing; 8.50 in-lb the second. Four children (17–24 months, 31–33.75 in, 21–28 lb; Danny was 17 months, 29.5 in, 25 lb) hanging on a bar with a torque gauge produced 79–148 above-threshold values in only 1.7–5.7 minutes; peaks 12–18 in-lb. Knobs didn't spring back, so small repeated rotations could add up.
- Observation: all 6 children showed rail-hanging, climbing, knob-turning; one jumping toddler rotated a rail a lot in 6 seconds; lifting the hinge made rotation even easier.
- Conclusions: Danny likely could rotate the rails. Any product for children must be TESTED WITH CHILDREN. Uses change with familiarity and context; products may have several users with competing goals: to the child, a crib is "a cage to escape, a knob to twist, a railing to hang on, a net to climb". Simple observation (within 30 seconds!) reveals a lot — sophisticated tools can't replace a broad approach.

# T27 Visual Ergonomics in Bus Stand and Aircraft Interior (Case Study)

Source for the aircraft part: Marstall, Miller & Poisson, "Collaboration in the Cockpit: Human–System Interaction Beyond the Autopilot" (Ergonomics in Design, Jan 2016). The bus-stand part is a class case study — apply the course principles (template below).

## A. Aircraft interior: the cockpit near-disaster
- Night flight in a two-seat F-4 Phantom II, dark overcast, no ground lights, level at 24,000 ft. A radio call came; the back-seater said "I've got it" and shook the stick (normal handover signal). The pilot assumed "it" meant the aircraft and looked down to write on his kneepad — but the other pilot only meant the radio information and had accidentally bumped the stick. NOBODY was flying.
- Wingman: "Lead, where are you going?" — the plane was upside down, diving through 15,000 ft at 400+ knots. Recovered just in time in a narrow valley.
- Type 1 (unrecognised) SPATIAL DISORIENTATION: the inner-ear semicircular canals can't sense rotation slower than 2°/s², so neither pilot felt the slow roll.
- USAF: 72 spatial-disorientation mishaps since 1993 → 101 lives, 65 aircraft, $2.32 billion.
- Root cause: communication — the standard handover protocol requires confirming ("You've got the aircraft"); tandem seating (one behind the other, for aerodynamics) removed the visual cue of seeing each other.

## Traditional attitude indicator problems
- Sperry attitude indicator (1929): artificial horizon — blue sky, brown ground, white horizon line, yellow dot = nose; like looking through a hole in the nose.
- Controversy: should the HORIZON move (Doolittle: pilot and aircraft are one frame, so the aircraft symbol shouldn't move) or the AIRCRAFT symbol move (principle of the moving part — the part the pilot controls should move, as in reality)? US instruments arguably violate the principle of the moving part; training makes it hard to change.
- Needs FOVEAL fixation — unlike real visual flight where the horizon is seen in peripheral vision → checked less often, competes with other central-vision tasks.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_026.jpg|Traditional Sperry-style attitude indicator: nose down, 30° left bank]

## Redesign: a context-aware "artificial wingman"
- Goals: (a) use context (e.g., "cruise" phase should be straight and level) to give AUDIBLE alerts of unexpected attitude, so the pilot needn't stare at the instrument; (b) give real-time audible + visual recovery instructions.
- Display: aircraft symbol shown banked, a grey wedge enlarges the cue, command text "BANK RIGHT" (also spoken), green chevron pitch status pointer.
[FIG:fig/d/ve_027.jpg|Redesigned attitude stabilisation display with 'BANK RIGHT' command]
- Issues: pilots accept selecting a flight phase in normal flight, but not constant alarms in turbulence (more workload). Incessant repeated alarms get ignored; questions from a teammate are received better than commands. Spoken commands take >1 s to say, hear and act — in a fast roll the situation may have changed, so ALERT EARLY.
- Should the aircraft take control? Automatic Ground Collision Avoidance System recovers within 1.5 s of the point of no return. Fitts (1951): use human judgement, but machines sense faster. A 2013 pilot, disoriented at night, ejected and died — displays needing foveal fixation are not enough in time-critical stress.
- Lessons: standardised two-way human–computer communication (like the pilots' handover protocol), systems that understand long-term goals and only speak up for unexpected events, tone of alerts, human limits.

## Common principles that appear again and again (use in any answer)

1. Know thy user — elderly, children, stressed, untrained, colour-blind users.

2. Visibility → legibility → readability (size, contrast, case, spacing, typeface).

3. Redundancy — colour + shape + text + sound + touch.

4. Consistency and standardisation — same symbol, same place, same meaning.

5. Simplicity and decluttering — 3–4 units of info, no irrelevant content.
