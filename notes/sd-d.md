# UNIT 1 — SERVICES (Lectures 1 & 2)

# 1.1 Design of SYSTEMS and SERVICES

## What designers deal with

As designers we work with two kinds of systems:

1. Real-world systems ARTIFICIALLY CREATED BY HUMANS for their benefit (railways, hospitals, banks, a college).

2. The ITC (information & communication technology) systems at the BACK that RUN these real-world systems (the booking software, hospital records system).
- Designers DESIGN NEW systems, RENOVATE or INNOVATE, and CONTINUOUSLY UPGRADE EXISTING ones.
- By structure, systems can be: simple or complex; independent or sub-systems; connected, integrated or embedded; free-standing or nested.

## What is a service?
- Services are ORGANISED, COORDINATED ACTIVITIES providing and facilitating NEED FULFILMENT to living beings.
- Many services are DESIGNED and OPERATED as SYSTEMS. (The course first introduces services, then systems.)
- A service is an INTANGIBLE activity (or set of activities) designed to solve a specific problem, fulfil a particular need, or provide some value to the end user.
- Examples: opening a bank account, ordering a taxi, booking a flight, drinking a cup of tea at a café, getting admitted to IIIT Jabalpur for the first time.
- Using a service usually means the customer INTERACTS with a SERVICE PROVIDER — a real person (customer-support agent), a product or system (app, software, chatbot), or both.

## Product design vs service design

| Product | Service |
| Something I OWN and use | Something I USE but do NOT own |
| One-to-one interaction | One-to-many interaction |
| Designer can control it precisely | Many actors and conditions — designer can't control everything; IxD leading to UX is complex |
| Can exist in ISOLATION | CANNOT exist in isolation — needs people, processes, partners |
| Product-centric design: value exchanged through the product | Service-centric design: value created in the EXPERIENCE and interactions over time |
| Focus: building the right product (discover, define, build, measure); more control, faster cycles | Focus: designing holistic experiences and coordinating systems (people, processes, technology, partners, touchpoints, policy); many dependencies, long-term impact |

**Example:** A pressure cooker is a product — you buy it and own it. A Zomato order is a service — app, restaurant, rider, payment gateway, support team must all work together every single time.

## When a service becomes STRUCTURAL — three conditions

1. COORDINATION — multiple actors must align.

2. CONTINUITY — outcomes must hold over time.

3. TRUST — because most of the system is INVISIBLE to the user.
- Healthcare, banking and government are not just sectors — they are LARGE-SCALE SERVICE SYSTEMS that guarantee outcomes individuals cannot produce alone.

## The Service Cycle

A service is used REPEATEDLY, so it moves in a cycle:

ENTICE → (attract) → ENTER → (orient) → EXPERIENCE → (use) → EXIT → (retain) → EXTEND → back to entice.
- Most services are repetitive and cyclic → CONSISTENCY + RELIABILITY are crucial.
- Challenge: each time, the UX must be CONSISTENT and also NOVEL ("How to make it exciting and novel each and every time?").
- Service design considers the END-TO-END experience; it is not just designing the service itself — it is broader and holistic, involving HUMAN AGENTS and their BEHAVIOUR.

**Example:** A Café Coffee Day visit — ads/offers entice you, you enter and are oriented (menu board), you order and drink (experience/use), you pay and leave (exit), a loyalty app brings you back (retain), you try a new drink or bring friends (extend).

# 1.2 What is Service Design?

## Formal definition

Service design is the practice of PLANNING and ORGANISING the PEOPLE, INFRASTRUCTURE and PROCESSES of a service so that the experience is COHERENT for the customer and EFFICIENT for the business.
- OBJECTIVE: improve services to make them more USEFUL, USABLE and DESIRABLE for clients, and more EFFICIENT and EFFECTIVE for organisations.
- It treats each service as a SYSTEM — designing the customer-facing moments (FRONT END / frontstage) together with the BACKSTAGE (back end) operations that produce them.
- OUTPUT: a coordinated set of implementable designs (documents, mock-ups, metrics) that DESCRIBE and SPECIFY how the service should work, plus the operational changes needed to deliver it.
- Design of a service = OPTIMISING the QUALITY and DELIVERY of a service, both for the end user (customer) and for the service providers.
- It considers PEOPLE, PROPS (touchpoints) and PROCESSES involved in delivering a service, so the experience is high-quality for customers AND for those providing it.
- Service design is a HOLISTIC process for CREATING NEW services and IMPROVING EXISTING ones.
- Service Design field = UI + UX + IxD + CX + PD (product design) + Design Management.
- "These don't happen by chance or luck. They have to be built in to be successful."

## Service = exchange of value via experience

PROVIDER —provides→ SERVICE ← pays — CUSTOMER. The provider puts in money, labour/work, information, material/product, infrastructure and energy/utility; the customer gets value through the EXPERIENCE.

## How service design works

It is a human-centred, collaborative, interdisciplinary, ITERATIVE approach that uses RESEARCH, PROTOTYPING and VISUALISATION tools to create specified experiences that meet the needs of the business, the user and other stakeholders.

## Examples of services shown in class
- Platform services (Uber-type): rides, delivery, groceries, car-sharing, micromobility, business.
- Consumer services: salon & spa, fitness centres, travel agencies, house cleaning, pet care, home repair, event planning, tutoring, laundry & dry cleaning, virtual assistance, catering, car rental.
- Health Delivery System of India: 3 tiers — primary, secondary, tertiary.
- Travel & hospitality; types of e-commerce (B2B, B2C, C2C, C2B, B2G etc.).
- Citizen–government services: service EXPERIENCE (what citizens feel), TOUCHPOINTS (web, phone, in person, community, third party, in-home), DELIVERY (staff, programmes, performance, process), FOUNDATIONS (legislation, policy, IT, space, standards, partners), MANDATE (direction, commitments, values, principles). Citizens' needs and expectations at the top, government decisions and actions at the bottom.
- Community services (animal shelters, park clean-ups, homeless shelters) and in-service education.
- Types of business services: banking, insurance, transportation, warehousing, communication, postal, telecom.

# 1.3 Characteristics, challenges and elements of services

## Four service characteristics (and why they are hard to design)

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
| Intangible | Cannot be touched or stored; judged by experience | A haircut, an online class |
| Simultaneous production and consumption | Made and used at the same time, with the customer present | A doctor's consultation |
| Heterogeneous | Varies every time (different staff, mood, day) | Two waiters serve differently |
| Perishable | Cannot be stored for later | An empty train seat today is lost forever |

## Risks in describing services

1. Oversimplification — inadequate to describe complex service systems.

2. Incompleteness — omitting details of unfamiliar service elements.

3. Subjectivity — description biased by personal experience.

4. Biased interpretation — the same outcome understood differently by different people.

## Service design elements (role of a Design Manager)

| Group | Element | Topics |
| STRUCTURAL | Delivery system | Process structure, service blueprint, strategic positioning |

|   | Capacity planning | Strategic role, queuing models, planning criteria |
| MANAGERIAL | Information | Technology, scalability, use of the internet |
|   | Quality | Measurement, design quality, recovery, tools, six-sigma |
|   | Service encounter | Encounter triad, culture, supply relationships, outsourcing |
|   | Managing capacity and demand | Strategies, yield management, queue management |

- There are hundreds of other UX points a designer must factor in. Example from class: a dry cleaner delivered clothes using the Metro but did not realise his container was bigger than the Metro permitted.

# 1.4 Five established principles of service design

These are not a PROCESS — they are CONSTRAINTS that shape research, synthesis and design decisions while designing systems that will deliver the service.

| Principle | Meaning | Example |
| 1. User-centred | Designed from the perspective of the PEOPLE WHO USE them; based on research, not assumptions; qualitative work with real customers | Talking to real patients before redesigning OPD registration |
| 2. Co-creative | STAKEHOLDERS ACROSS THE BUSINESS participate — frontline staff, backstage operators, ops, IT, legal; each holds information research alone won't reveal | Nurses, ward boys and IT staff in the workshop |
| 3. Sequencing | Services are SEQUENCES OF INTERRELATED ACTIONS across time, not isolated touchpoints | A great call-centre call can't save a bad onboarding three weeks earlier |
| 4. Evidencing | Services are INTANGIBLE, so artefacts/touchpoints make them visible — blueprints, journey maps, personas, prototypes let a team reason together | A printed journey map on the wall |
| 5. Holistic | The ENTIRE ECOSYSTEM is in scope — channels, actors, front and backstage, partners, wider context | Including the payment gateway and delivery partner |

## Commerce is not service
- A user pays and in return gets goods/services = a TRANSACTION → that is COMMERCE, not service.
- A service aims at investing in the EXPERIENCE AROUND the transaction.
- Hence friction exists: a service designer must convince Marketing and Sales, whose point of view may differ.

## Why is service design important? (Expectation vs experience)
- Customers compare EXPECTATIONS with EXPERIENCES. Match → satisfied. Expectation high, experience falls short → DISSATISFIED. Experience greater than expected → very satisfied, even DELIGHTED.
- Customer satisfaction is always a COMPARISON of these values: Pre-service (expectations) → Service (experiences) → Post-service (satisfaction).

## Service design vs "designing a service"
- Traditional design of a product or service focuses on AESTHETICS — what it looks or sounds like (front end, usability) — designed to PLEASE, satisfy, fulfil.
- Service design goes FAR BEYOND THE VISIBLE: it starts from "underground", not just the visible layer, and aims at experiences ending in DELIGHT. It goes beyond product design.
- Its core is to CHALLENGE and RESHAPE EVERYTHING — from operations to the business model of an organisation.

## Traditional marketing vs service design
- Marketing is SELLING; it interacts with consumers but focuses on sales and feedback. Marketing does not deal with personas.
- Service design is involved in the EXPERIENTIAL part — meeting customer expectations to attract new customers and hold existing ones; it can create PERSONAS and helps you KNOW your customers.
- Service design plans the PEOPLE, PROCESSES and TOUCHPOINTS behind a service so that it works for both customers and the organisation, FRONTSTAGE and BACKSTAGE.

## Origin story
- Service design as a named discipline traces back to LYNN SHOSTACK's 1982 Harvard Business Review article "HOW TO DESIGN A SERVICE". She argued services were being managed without a design discipline and introduced the SERVICE BLUEPRINT — the visual technique still used to map frontstage and backstage. This paper is treated as the founding moment.
- 1991: Köln International School of Design starts teaching service design; 2000s: practice spreads to government, healthcare, financial services; today: a global community with shared methods.

# 1.5 Service Design vs UX vs CX vs Product Design vs Design Thinking

These terms are used interchangeably by organisations that don't understand the role of design — that confusion costs teams real time. They overlap but differ in SCOPE, FOCUS, ARTEFACT and OWNER.

| Discipline | Scope | Primary focus | Key artefacts | Typical owner |
| Service design | End-to-end service across channels and over time, including backstage | Aligning frontstage and backstage | Service blueprints, journey maps, stakeholder maps | Design, operations, or dedicated service design / CX team |
| UX design | A specific digital product or interface | Usability and interaction quality | Wireframes, prototypes, interaction specs, usability tests | Design or product team |
| Product design | Features and capabilities of a product | Building the right product | Product specs and prototypes | Product |
| Design thinking | A problem-solving MINDSET and process | Method, not an artefact | Varies | Any function |
| Customer experience (CX) | Customer's overall perception across ALL interactions/touchpoints | Measurement, strategy, improvement of perceived experience | VoC (voice of customer) programmes, NPS, journey maps, CX strategy | CX, marketing, operations or executive |

- "Outside-in" view: concentric layers — experiences → interactions → touchpoints → procedures → systems.
- Nesting: SERVICE DESIGN ⊃ CX ⊃ UX ⊃ UI (service design contains all).
- Layers from STRATEGIC to OPERATIONAL: Service design → CX → UX → IxD → UI design (marketing & experience design at the top; product design at the bottom).
- CX–UX–UI is a "chicken and egg" question — "only a designer knows the answer".

## Service system layers

| Layer | Concerned with |
| Macro | Social system, policy making |
| Meso | Organisation strategy, collaborative behaviour |
| Micro | Person-to-person, operations |

Services at each layer are different. A business strategy framework helps the designer within a company to hasten change via novelty.

## Core tools (artefacts) of service design

Personas · customer journey maps · service blueprints · stakeholder maps and ecosystem maps · service prototypes (storyboards, role-play, walkthroughs, co-creation in/of physical spaces). Each is used at a different layer of the design problem. (Explained in Unit 3.)

# 1.6 The service ECOSYSTEM and research areas

## Services ecosystem
- Every service operates within an ECOSYSTEM that must be considered for setting BOUNDARY CONDITIONS when it is conceptualised.
- A mathematical model, if attempted, would have too many variables and almost no constants — so which variables should designers focus on?
- The service ecosystem ("more than the interface"):

| Part | Contents |
| Inputs | Political priorities, economic context, public/market expectations, regulation & compliance, societal needs, technology change |
| Centre | PEOPLE — users, citizens, customers, communities |
| Around people | Policy & governance; technology & data; partners & suppliers; institutions & culture; funding & resources; operations |
| Outcomes | Service outcomes, public/customer value, operational performance, equity & accessibility, trust & legitimacy, economic & social impact |
| Context layers (always shifting) | Political cycle, market forces, social change, technological evolution, environmental constraints, global events |

## Skills, cost and scale
- Skill pyramid: HIGH skill (education, knowledge-intensive services) → MEDIUM skill (business services, health & social, trade, transport, public administration) → LOW skill (agriculture, manufacturing, HORECA — hotels/restaurants/catering).
- Skills involved determine the COST of services; overheads keep shooting up because of uncertainty in the ecosystem.
- Margin per user (ROI) is HIGHER in skilled services.
- Services are usually designed to be SCALABLE in numbers, geographical reach and economic strata penetration.
- Strictly, designers should be given ecosystem information by other teams; if you have to develop the map yourself, do a SWOT. Ask: what EXTERNAL VARIABLES must be accounted for within the system?

## Service system research areas (Waseda University model)

| Area | Fields |
| Function realisation | Robotics, requirement engineering, software engineering, informatics |
| Provider/receiver interaction | Consensus building, interaction design |
| Service value | Value engineering, knowledge engineering, macro/micro economics, game theory, behaviour science, anthropology |
| Provider activities | Design, system engineering, OR/IE, quality management, cost management |
| Receiver activities | Marketing, behaviour science, Kansei engineering, human modelling, human management |
| Service provider environment | Human resources, education, building, IT infrastructure |
| Organisation science | Management science, system engineering, social science |
| Service system management | Policy, social system, platform |
| Environmental factors | Politics, society, economy, culture, civilisation |

## ITIL service lifecycle

Service STRATEGY at the centre, surrounded by service DESIGN → service TRANSITION → service OPERATION, all wrapped in CONTINUAL SERVICE IMPROVEMENT.

## CS/IT meaning of "systems design"

Computer science and IT use "systems design" to mean ONLY computer systems (system design frameworks, architecture, OSI model — 7 layers: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application). Creative designers must know basics if they want to interact with coding/IT teams; without this, using AI will be impossible.

(Or choose another service, e.g., laundry.) Team of 4. Use design-thinking steps — brainstorm, research, synthesis, prototype, test/validate, refine. Start by outlining the steps, referring to the IKEA Kitchen Planner service.

# UNIT 2 — SYSTEMS (Lectures 3 & 4)

# 2.1 What is a system?

## Definitions
- A SYSTEM is a collection of INTERRELATED COMPONENTS (subsystems) that function together to achieve some outcome (biological, computer, social system).
- An INFORMATION SYSTEM is a collection of interrelated components that COLLECT, PROCESS, STORE and PROVIDE as output the information needed to complete business tasks (e.g., payroll system).
- Working definition for designers: a system is an AGGREGATION/ASSEMBLAGE of OBJECTS/PARTS with RELATIONSHIPS between their ATTRIBUTES (attributes = properties of objects).
- An object of current interest within a system = ENTITY. A property of an entity = ATTRIBUTE.
- STATE of a system = description of all its entities, attributes and activities.

| System | Entities | Attributes | Activities |
| Traffic | Cars, scooters etc. — but if only cars interest us, only cars become entities | Speed, size, direction | Driving, parking, reversing |
| Bank | Customers | Accounts | Withdrawal, deposits |
| Inverter | Transformer, PCB, switches, relays, SMPS, batteries | Frequency, AC/DC, voltage, amps, heat | Storing, converting (AC→DC, DC→AC) |

- Any system can be BROKEN DOWN into its constituent objects/entities and analysed for their properties and interconnections = SYSTEMS ANALYSIS.

## Types of systems
- Natural: global climate, plant kingdom, solar system, human being.
- Artificial: transport, water, product, entertainment, furniture systems.
- Information systems: belong to both — today mostly computers and IT.

## State description of a system (pairs)

Complex vs simple · chaotic vs orderly · controlled vs self-governing · adaptive vs closed · evolving · static vs dynamic · continuous vs discrete.
- Describing the "level" and "nature" of a system is RELATIVE — it depends on the point of view.
- A system is a QUALITATIVE concept where the WHOLE IS MORE THAN THE SUM OF ITS PARTS.

## Levels of a system

| Level | Natural example | Digital example |
| Meta systems | Universe | E-governance; semantic search engines, LML |
| Super systems | Galaxy | Search engines |
| Systems | Solar system | Network clusters (ERNET) |
| Sub systems | Earth & Moon | LAN, websites |
| Objects/components/entities | Earth | Departments, HODs |

# 2.2 System modelling
- Real-world live/dynamic systems are complex, large, and not feasible to experiment with in reality → we build a MODEL.
- A MODEL is not only an ANALOGY for a system; it is also a SIMPLIFICATION.

| Model type | How it works | Example |
| User models | Persona; user UX journey | A persona + journey map of a student |
| Physical models | Simulation shown physically | Scale model of a bus stand |
| Mathematical models | Simulation represented symbolically (IT, computer science) | Queue equations for a ticket counter |

- There is NO UNIQUE model of a system; different models emerge from different analyses. There are no unique standards for VISUAL model representation in design — designers are WELCOME TO ENRICH models visually: graphics, animation, motion design, colour.
- A system is a SET of objects with RELATIONSHIPS between their attributes. Objects have attributes (properties); relationships between attributes BIND the system.
- Any change in an attribute of an object affects the relationship, which in turn affects the ENTIRE SYSTEM. Relationships act like LINKS.
- In information systems (OOP — object-oriented programming), each object is self-contained with its own data and logic; an object belongs to a CLASS, and each class has methods that define behaviour.
- A system is characterised by a BOUNDARY that cuts across connecting links. The links define the external behaviour, so a FUNCTION can be written relating INPUTS and OUTPUTS.
- Graphical depiction: an oval boundary containing subsystems joined by interrelationships, with inputs and outputs; or a dashed boundary box with entities E1, E2, E3 feeding blocks.

## Deterministic vs stochastic

| Deterministic | Stochastic |
| Outcome of an activity fully described by its inputs | Effects vary randomly; outcomes governed by probability |
| A calculator: same input, same output | A food-delivery time: traffic, weather, rider |

- MOST SYSTEMS IN DESIGN BEHAVE STOCHASTICALLY (dynamic, involve randomness), but users EXPECT deterministic behaviour — designers must classify them otherwise.

# 2.3 Characteristics of systems, systems analysis and systems thinking

## Characteristics
- Made up of INTERRELATED SUBSYSTEMS (a nuclear reactor = boilers, reactor components, etc.).
- FUNCTIONAL DECOMPOSITION — dividing a system into components based on subsystems, which are further divided.
- SYSTEM BOUNDARY — separation between a system and its environment, where inputs and outputs cross.
- AUTOMATION BOUNDARY — separation between the automated part and the manual part (AI integration).
- Designers should know how to ANALYSE, CONCEPTUALISE, MODEL and PROTOTYPE systems — digital and real.

## Key terms
- SYSTEM ANALYSIS: understanding and specifying in detail WHAT the information system should do (broken into objects/entities, analysed for properties and interconnections).
- SYSTEM DESIGN: specifying in detail HOW the many component parts should be implemented.
- SYSTEM ANALYST: a professional who uses analysis and design techniques to solve business problems with IT. Increasingly, design managers are becoming system analysts in IT companies (food-delivery apps, taxi services).

## "Systems thinking"

Being able to identify something as a system (cluster parts as a system) · identify subsystems · identify characteristics and functions · identify where the boundaries are (or should be) · identify inputs and outputs · identify relationships among subsystems · identify data patterns and now AI TOKENS.
- Systems thinking is not just for designing; it ensures design is practised across stakeholders in a company. Enterprise architecture layers: BUSINESS layer (purpose & goal, competency, service, process) → INFORMATION layer (application, data) → TECHNOLOGY layer (platform, infrastructure). UX sits where technology, business and design overlap.

## Systems thinking vs conventional thinking

| Conventional thinking | Systems thinking |
| Connection between problems and causes is obvious and easy to trace | Relationship is indirect and not obvious |
| Others (inside or outside our organisation) are to blame and must change | We unwittingly create our own problems and can solve them by changing our behaviour |
| A short-term policy will also assure long-term success | Most quick fixes have unintended consequences — no difference or worse in the long run |
| To optimise the whole, optimise the parts | To optimise the whole, improve RELATIONSHIPS among the parts |
| Tackle many independent initiatives simultaneously | A few key coordinated changes sustained over time produce large system change |

- Roots of systems thinking: systems theory (Bertalanffy, Foerster, Haken), economics (Gomez, Probst, Beer, Dänzer), sociology (Hajek, Luhmann), ecology (Capra, Meadows, Vester), communication theory (Bateson, Watzlawick), conflict & peace studies (Galtung, Rapoport), epistemology (Dörner, Maturana, Varela), chaos theory (Feigenbaum, Mandelbrot, Prigogine).

**Example:** Traffic jams near a college gate — conventional thinking blames drivers; systems thinking sees class timings, auto stands, parking, signal timing and footpaths together, and fixes the relationships (staggered timings, drop-off bay).

## The designer as Usability Engineer (ISO)

A designer working in HCI is a "usability engineer". Responsibilities:

1. Identifying and gathering user requirements.

2. Specifying and defining usability factors and goals.

3. Modelling and designing user interactions.

4. Designing user interfaces (GUI, icons, web graphics, animations, menus).

5. Conducting heuristic evaluations, protocol analysis, contextual enquiries and task analysis.

6. Formulating, testing and certifying usage and visual quality of interfaces.

7. Team-working with cross-functional software teams throughout product definition, development and user feedback.

# 2.4 Information systems
- Information systems are the core of all digital/virtual products used by society, groups or individuals for efficiency, cost-effectiveness and pleasure.
- Company-level SUPERSYSTEM example: All information systems → Customer Relationship Management (sales management, account management, product information, technical support systems) + accounting & financial management + manufacturing management + human resources management.
- Components of an information system: PEOPLE, PROCEDURES, DATA, SOFTWARE, HARDWARE (+ inputs and outputs).
- Front end vs back end vs FULL STACK. The DESIGNER is responsible for the FRONT END; the IT professional for the BACK END. How do they communicate design specifications to integrate both? A new specialist — the FULL-STACK DEVELOPER — is a high-demand job. Embedded full stack: drivers, RTOS, middleware, application logic, cloud/IoT gateway.

## Types of information systems (by level) — Library example

| Level | User | Library example |
| Strategic | Director | Budget, total books, future expansion, digitisation, new building, new staff |
| Tactical | Librarian | Daily borrowings, tracking heavy demand, loss of books, staff on duty, orders, payments, cash flow, senate instructions |
| Operating | Issuing clerk, deputy librarian, student | Book lending/receipts, accounts, requests, notices, new additions, daily statistics, day-to-day operations |

- The data is organised as a HIERARCHY of information; in business it reflects the business framework of the organisation.
- A modern IS must be designed for future expansion, flexibility, integrity across levels, easy maintenance, modularity, security, financial viability, a common database, error-free working, reliability, and usability by the common user.

## Four types of IS by function

| Type | What it does | Example |
| Transaction Processing System (TPS) | Captures and records transactions | A sale at a shop, an ATM withdrawal |
| Management Information System (MIS) | Uses TPS data to produce reports for planning and control | Monthly sales report |
| Executive Information System (EIS) | Information for strategic planning, from internal databases or outside sources | Stock-market reports for the CEO |
| Decision Support System (DSS) | Supports decisions; explore "what if" options; related to expert/knowledge-based systems (AI) | "What if we open a new branch?" |

## Skills of a systems analyst
- Technical: computers and how they work; programming languages; devices that interact with computers; communication networks; database and DBMS; operating systems and utilities. Tools: MS Access, IDEs, CASE tools.
- Business: what activities and processes organisations perform; how they are structured and managed; type of work (hospital, bank); who the "actors" are. About the specific organisation: what it does, what makes it successful, its strategies and plans, its tradition (culture) and values.

## Typical job titles

| ITC domain | Creative design–tech domain |

# 3.2 Personas
- A PERSONA is a FICTIONAL PROFILE that represents a type of user, customer or audience you want to understand better.
- Usually includes: name and photo; demographics (age, occupation, location); GOALS; CHALLENGES/frustrations; personality and habits; motivations; social channels.
- Why personas matter: FOCUS (who you're talking to), EMPATHY (see from the user's point of view), CONSISTENCY (every piece of content/feature aligns with real needs), BETTER COMMUNICATION (team shares one understanding).
- How to create: research your audience; observe or survey real people; look for similarities in goals, frustrations, lifestyles.
- Examples from slides: Maya Sharma, 24, student & book blogger (goal: build an online community around reading; challenge: balancing study with content creation). Quick example: Karan, 20-year-old engineering student who wants time-saving study tools. Buyer persona "Daniela Vargas" (marketing manager; personality sliders introvert–extrovert, sensing–intuition, thinking–feeling, judging–perceiving; motivations; frustrations; social channels).
- Personas can be built on a WALL with sticky notes in a workshop.

## 10 steps to personas (Lene Nielsen)

1. Collect data. 2. Form a hypothesis. 3. Everyone accepts the hypothesis. 4. Establish the number of personas. 5. Construct and describe the personas. 6. Prepare situations/scenarios for the personas. 7. Obtain acceptance from the organisation. 8. Disseminate knowledge. 9. Create scenarios. 10. Make ongoing adjustments.

Example persona for the laptop-repair mini project: "Prof. Mehta, 48, HOD at an engineering college in Jabalpur; 60 lab computers; goal: zero downtime during exams; frustration: service centre is 12 km away and needs three visits."

# 3.4 Customer journey maps and user story mapping

## Journey map (also called user journey mapping)
- Existing system: map how a USER experiences the PRESENT service/context.
- New system: map how a user NEEDS TO BE GUIDED/NAVIGATED to experience a specified set of experiences — how he/she is made to feel at each stage, how they sequentially navigate; the mental model must flow with the real-world model in terms of evoked EMOTIONS, so that positive feelings remain.
- There is NO STANDARD way to illustrate these maps — it is left to the designer: a good opportunity to present it as a STORY and use all strengths of graphic design and visualisation.
- Components of a customer journey map: review goals → gather research → empathy map → brainstorm touchpoints & channels → brainstorm with team → sketch the journey → refine & digitise → share & use.
- Typical rows (online travel agency example): stages of the journey, customer needs & activities, expectations, EMOTIONAL STATE (happy–sad curve), opportunities for improvement.
- Guest experience map (Booking.com): clusters of points of satisfaction, dissatisfaction and delight across a hotel stay.

## User story mapping
- Template: TIME runs left → right. Top row = USER ACTIVITIES (the "backbone"); below = USER TASKS (the "walking skeleton"); below that = USER STORIES, ordered by priority and sliced horizontally into RELEASES (Release 1, 2, 3).
- Concepts: users, user tasks, goal level, activities, backbone, narrative flow, release slice, details, "map now and later", "map the whole system".

## Combined artefact

One sheet can combine a PERSONA, story line, user journey and stakeholder map (persona data, scenario, goals & expectations, supporting characters; journey actions — research, evaluation, action, validation; feelings & thoughts; opportunities).

## Stakeholder map

# 3.6 The Service Blueprint

## What it is
- A service blueprint maps TASKS AS THEY HAPPEN OR ARE SUPPOSED TO HAPPEN. Similar to a journey map but goes MUCH DEEPER — gives insight into the company.
- A blueprint is a PERSONA's experience through a SINGLE service journey; it shows a use case/path over time. Additional blueprints are needed for variations.
- ALTITUDE: HIGH-altitude (one-page overview, starting point) vs LOW-altitude (detailed — an invaluable management tool once all IT systems and support processes are identified).

## Components (rows) of a service blueprint

| Row | Meaning | Restaurant example |
| Physical evidence | Props and places the customer sees | Menu card, table, bill |
| 1. Customer actions | All steps, choices, interactions the customer performs to reach a goal (taken from the journey map) | Customer orders food |
| — Line of interaction — | Direct interactions between customer and company |   |
| 2. Frontstage (visible/onstage) contact employee actions | Actions visible to the customer | Waiter takes the order |
| — Line of visibility — | Separates what the customer sees from what they don't |   |
| 3. Backstage (invisible) contact employee actions | Behind the scenes, supporting the onstage actions | Chef prepares the meal |
| — Line of internal interaction — | Where contact staff interact with support |   |
| 4. Support processes | Internal services, steps, systems and partners that support employees | Inventory system, supplier delivers vegetables |

- Extended rows (Izac Ross's anatomy): QUALITY MEASURES (how is success measured?), EMOTIONAL JOURNEY (customer's emotional state), TIME (e.g., 00:00:00 → 00:00:30), physical evidence (props, place), customer action (experiences, thinking, actions), frontstage: service employees, digital & device; backstage: employees, apps, data, networks; support processes: partners, people, process.
- Legend: arrow crossing a line = VALUE EXCHANGE; arrow direction = CONTROL/DEPENDENCY; double arrow = AGREEMENT must be reached to move the process forward; "creativity required" = service staff have a mandate for creative production.
- Digital example rows: time (10–30 min, 20–40 min…), evidence (website, email receipt, credit-card receipt), customer journey, frontstage employee actions, technology (support chat, payment provider), backstage actions, support processes.

## Building a service blueprint — 6 steps

1. Identify the process to be blueprinted.

2. Identify the customer segments.

3. Map processes from the customer's point of view.

4. Map contact employee actions (onstage and backstage) and/or technology.

5. Link contact activities to the needed support functions.

6. Add evidence of service at each customer action step.

## Journey map vs service blueprint

| Journey map | Service blueprint |
| Shows the experience from the USER'S perspective | Shows how the service is delivered BEHIND THE SCENES |
| Focuses on what the user does, thinks, feels, experiences | Focuses on processes, people, systems and how work gets done |
| Shows user actions & touchpoints, emotions & pain points, frontstage interactions, experience over time | Shows frontstage actions (what users see), backstage processes (what they don't), supporting systems & technologies, people/roles/handoffs, dependencies & failures |
| Best for understanding user experience and finding opportunities | Best for understanding how the service works, finding bottlenecks, improving delivery |
| Example: search for info → fill application → wait for response → receive outcome (emotion curve dips while waiting) | Same example with rows: User (search, apply, wait, receive) / Frontstage (show info, capture details, check status, display outcome) / Backstage (validate details, assess application, make decision, update records) / Supporting systems (database, server, records, email) |

- "Journey map = the user's experience; service blueprint = the service behind the experience. Great services are designed with both."

# 3.7 Task flows, flowcharts and service prototypes
- A service blueprint is connected to back-end computing systems through TASK FLOWS, which interact across the LINE OF INTERACTION.
- TASK FLOW diagrams are the FIRST visual modelling you have to master. Examples: "Finding a service" (landing → choose category → filter & sort → list of services → description); "Booking a slot" (view available slots → book → review → confirm).
- Flowchart examples: ordering a book from Amazon; a login system; making tea with a vending machine (start → take paper cup → place under opening → decision "cup placed?" → check beverage → press button → remove cup → stop).
- Flowchart symbols: START/END (oval), INPUT/OUTPUT (parallelogram), PROCESS (rectangle), DECISION (diamond).
- SWIM LANES: used when more than one actor or event is involved (customer, sales, contracts, legal, fulfilment). Task-flow diagrams also show HIERARCHY, SEQUENCE and HYPERTEXT structures. Heavily used in usability research and software development.
- Service prototypes: storyboards, role-play, walkthroughs, co-creation in or of physical spaces.

# 3.8 Design systems and system-design basics (awareness level)

## How to create a design system in Figma (7 steps)

1. Define foundations (TOKENS): colour, text styles, spacing grid, radius, shadows.

2. Build base components: buttons, input fields, checkboxes, cards.

3. Create variants & states: default, hover, active, disabled.

4. Build patterns (compositions): forms, tables, card lists.

5. Document & guide: usage, do's and don'ts, best practices, accessibility.

6. Publish as a library.

7. Govern & scale: versioning, change log, contributors, sync with developers, audits.

## System design quick guide (what back-end teams talk about)

- Patterns: microservices, event-driven, circuit breaker, backend-for-frontend, bulkhead. Reliability: health checks, failover, retry & timeout, chaos testing, auto-scaling. Monitoring: dashboards, alerting, logging, metrics, tracing.

# 4.4 Symbolic notation: context diagrams, DFDs and E-R diagrams

Notations adopted by ICT for modelling: CONTEXT DIAGRAMS, DOCUMENT FLOW DIAGRAMS, DATA FLOW DIAGRAMS (DFDs), ENTITY–RELATIONSHIP (E-R) DIAGRAMS.

## DFD elements

| Element | Symbol (Gane & Sarson / DeMarco & Yourdon) | Rules |
| PROCESS | Rounded rectangle / CIRCLE | Has a number, a name (verb phrase), description, one or more inputs and outputs |
| DATA FLOW | Arrow | Name (noun), description, one or more connections to a process |
| DATA STORE | Open-ended rectangle with "D1" | Number, name (noun), input and output data flows |
| EXTERNAL ENTITY | Rectangle/square | Name (noun), description |

## Document flow → logical flow
- Different tasks involve different PROCESSES within which DATA FLOWS. Groups of tasks form PROCESSES/NODES; when interconnected they show how data flows → a DFD. The DATA/DOCUMENT PROCESS FLOW DIAGRAM is drawn by ITERATION.
- Organisations may have only documents (not computerised), partly documents and partly computerised, or only computerised (paperless).
- Next step: convert the flow of documents into its representation on computers. Programs operate on LOGIC & ALGORITHMS, so field activity is captured digitally as LOGICAL FLOW DIAGRAMS (LDFDs).
- Designers cannot afford mistakes here — it will show up as their fault. They are specialists in HCD, trained in user-behaviour research and HCI, and must HANDSHAKE back-end engineers with front-end interactions.
- Example (goods received): DFD — vendor → delivery note → receiving office → items + note → inspection office; discrepancy note → purchase office. LDFD — vendor's delivery note → process "compare delivery with ledger entry" (ledger with order) → items received note → inspection office; discrepancy note → purchase office. (Difference: the LDFD shows processes as circles and data stores, not offices passing paper.)

## Context diagram — the FIRST step in service designing
- The word CONTEXT is critical in designing systems on the field and also in IT (in AI, prompt engineering needs CONTEXT so AI understands your intention).
- The bird's-eye view of a real system; the first figure in every business process; shows the context into which the business process fits.
- Shows the whole business process as JUST ONE PROCESS (process 0) and all EXTERNAL ENTITIES that receive information from or contribute information to the system. It draws the first BOUNDARY of the system against its ecosystem.
- How: start by drawing the main process circle defining the context; list the actors/entities who run the system; connect with labelled data flows.
- Examples: Student course registration (student, course, instructor, dean, department, registrar); Railway reservation 0-level DFD (passenger, admin); Budget monitoring system (departments, management, suppliers).
- MESS MANAGEMENT SYSTEM context diagram: Students (rebate/extras note, payments ↔ bills), Vendors (requisitions, payments ↔ supplies), Mess Secretary (daily rate, menu), Chief Warden (overdue bills), Mess Manager (items needed each day, perishable items).

## Levelling (expanding the DFD)
- After the context diagram, draw the DFD for the entire system by EXPANDING it, depending on the number of processes, by referring to the SERVICE BLUEPRINT (breaking the system into manageable parts).
- Hostel mess expanded DFD: 1 Billing system; 2 Stores issue & control system; 3 Perishable ordering.
- LEVELLING = expanding a process into lower layers while keeping relationships intact: Process 1 (billing) → 1.1 calculate daily rate, 1.2 calculate students' bills, 1.3 reconcile payments, 1.4 find number of meals… Process 2 and 3 are levelled similarly. More complex system → more levelling (modelled on a whiteboard).
- Class quiz: Study the DFD for 5 minutes and reproduce it — How many processes? How many entities (notice MIRRORING — no new entity can be added)? How many databases? If, on the last day, the Director wants the Registrar added, can we add it at level 3 or must we restart at level 0? (Answer: a new EXTERNAL ENTITY can't appear at a lower level — it must be added at the context level.)

## 17 conventions (rules) for DFDs

1. No process that is a PURE DECISION.

2. No LOOPS.

3. A data flow must not be split into flows with different names and meanings.

4. No crossing lines.

5. Develop TOP-DOWN, starting with the context diagram.

6. Data must be CONSERVED — a process cannot create new data; it only transforms input into output.

7. Data retrieved from a file must have been stored in it earlier.

8. Data flows don't act as signals to activate processes; processes act on data, data cannot trigger processes.

9. A DFD does NOT show the sequence of steps; it is NOT a flowchart (no decision steps).

10. A data flow cannot connect two external entities directly.

11. When a process numbered p is levelled into n processes, they are named p.1 … p.n.

12. All data flows ENTERING a process must also enter its levelled DFD.

13. All data flows LEAVING a process must also leave its levelled DFD (data conserved).

14. New files (databases) may appear at higher (more detailed) levels.

15. No new EXTERNAL ENTITIES at levelled DFDs — entities are not parts of processes, so they can't expand or disappear.

16. Processes at each level should not exceed SEVEN.

17. Every process must have at least ONE INPUT and ONE OUTPUT.

## Mapping a system with a DFD — small example

Situation: Stores receive demand from a supply vendor to be paid for items delivered. Task: issue instructions to the Accountant to pay the Vendor. Two actors: Vendor (EXTERNAL entity) and Accountant (INTERNAL entity).
- Vendor → invoice → process "Stores issuing approval" (check if material delivered at store; if so inform accountant) → approved bill → Accountant; process updates the DATABASE (inventory register) after issuing the order.
- Designer's questions: What if more than one vendor? Two vendors with the same name? Multiple orders with multiple invoices on the same or different dates? How do you as a designer resolve such issues in UI-UX-IxD?

| (iii) Stores section issuing daily items to the Kitchen Manager | Menu from mess committee → calculate stores needed → issue items, update stock register → low-stock advice → check stock register → order to vendor; leftover note from kitchen |

4. Stage 2: derive LOGICAL FLOW DIAGRAMS from these document flows (stores process, purchase process, accounts process) → context diagram → expanded DFD → levelling.

# 4.6 E-R (Entity–Relationship) diagrams
- In DFDs, PROCESSES are the focus; in E-R diagrams, DATA is the focus. E-R diagrams represent ENTITIES and the RELATIONSHIPS between them.

| Element | Symbol | Meaning |
| ENTITY | Rectangle | Distinct real-world items in an application — NOUNS (Vendor, Item, Order) |
| RELATIONSHIP | Diamond | Meaningful dependencies — VERBS (supplies, placed for, placed with) |
| ATTRIBUTE | Oval / list | Properties of entities and relationships |

- Examples: Vendor —supplies— Items; Orders —placed for— Items and —placed with— Vendors.
- E-R diagrams model the ATTRIBUTES, which relate to information in the DATABASE: Vendor (vendor code, name, address); Supplies (vendor code, item code, order no., quantity supplied, date of supply, price/unit); Items (item code, item name); Orders (order no., order date); Placed for (order no., item code); Placed with (order no., vendor code, item code, qty ordered, price/unit, delivery time allowed).
- E-R diagrams model relationships by defining CARDINALITY: 1:1, 1:N, N:M (e.g., N vendors supply M items).
- Purpose for designers: HANDSHAKE with the BACK-END developer for DATABASE design; the designer needs E-R diagrams to seek collaboration from the IT team to get the final design AS THE DESIGNER WANTS IT; INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE depends on E-R. E-R is also used to conceptualise UI SCREEN SEQUENCES (order burger → want fries? → want drink? → pay cashier). Information swim lanes and information architecture are related.
- E-R modelling helps create new databases, connect existing ones and remodel database design. Types of attributes (simple, composite, single, multi-valued, derived, key), specialisation/generalisation and extended E-R are ADVANCED topics — an IT expert will do this (you too after practice). An overall view is sufficient for this course.
- Related awareness slides: data structures (array, linked list, stack, queue, tree, graph, hash table); data models (hierarchical, network, relational, E-R, object-oriented, semantic); how an LLM works end-to-end (tokens → embeddings → transformer layers → prediction) and LLM workflow patterns (chain, parallel, routing, orchestrator–workers, evaluator–optimiser).

# 4.7 UML, OOP and AI agents
- UML is ANOTHER SYSTEM of modelling services. Processes are drawn as ovals (USE CASES); ENTITIES are called ACTORS or AGENTS (stick figures).
- USE-CASE DIAGRAM examples: College registration system (use cases: student registration, manage examination, manage question bank, system login, test, reports; actors: student, administrator). ACME Bank system (open account, close account, select account, login, withdraw, deposit, "insufficient funds" extends withdraw, third-party deposit; actors: bank manager, customer, third party). Bank management system (admin, customer: manage customer info, manage applicants' accounts, withdrawing & saving transactions, record all transactions).
- OOP CHART: classes (tables) such as Suppliers, Products, Customers, Addresses, Customer_Orders, Payment_Methods, Product_Prices, Order_Delivery, each with attributes (customer_id, order_id…) and links — a consequence of developing a UML-based model.
- From workflows to AI agents: NON-AGENTIC workflow (user input → LLM → output); AGENTIC workflow (plan, use tools, evaluate, revise, deliver); AI AGENTS (receive task → understand environment → define goal → plan path → execute actions → evaluate outcome → feedback loop → deliver). Agentic design patterns: multi-agent, self-reflection, tool use, CodeAct, agentic RAG, ReAct. "AI in business" blueprint: predictive AI/ML, perception AI, generative AI, autonomous single-task agents, multi-agent orchestration.

# 4.8 Systems approach to problem solving (7 steps)

1. Define THE problem.

2. Understand the system and define it (entities, objects, boundaries, features, activities).

3. Find out the VARIABLES of the system.

4. How are the variables RELATED to each other?

5. What CHOICES are there to improve the system?

6. What are their COSTS (ROI + ROB) and risk level?

7. RE-CONFIGURE the components/subsystems according to the new concept.

**Example (applying to the laptop pick-up service):** 1 problem = institutional downtime; 2 system = institution, franchise centre, pickup staff, logistics, parts supplier, app; 3 variables = pickup time, repair time, parts availability, cost; 4 relations = parts delay → longer repair; 5 choices = loaner laptops, on-site minor repair, scheduled pickups; 6 cost and risk of each; 7 reconfigure = app booking + daily pickup route + loaner pool.

| ✎ Exam tip: Learn the 7 steps exactly; illustrate with the mini-project. |
